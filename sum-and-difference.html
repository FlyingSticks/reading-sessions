Sum and difference: two forms of one dissection

Euclid's theorem adds parts to make a whole. Minkowski's subtracts a part from a whole. Both forms can be drawn from the same square, on the same sheet, before either geometry's absolute is chosen. The absolute only decides what the areas stand for.

Difference · above the horizon

A₂ (whole, outside the overlap)—
A₁ + A₃ (the two parts)—
A₂ − A₁ = A₃—
Two triangles on a parallelogram's diagonal have equal area. Sliding the apex P along the base keeps the moving triangle's area. Subtract the overlap from both and the remainders agree. The form is whole minus part.

Harmonic · the frame on the horizon

(A, C; B, D) on the horizon—
A · C (from M, in r²)—
(A′, C′; B′, D′) on the time axis—
A₃ = C₂ (apexes at ±A from the horizon)—
C′ and A′ are the ends of the vertical diameter. Sight lines from SP through them meet the horizon at C and A. Because the two diameters are perpendicular, SP sees C′B and BA′ under 45°, and A, B, C, D fall harmonically. The perspectivity from SP carries the set to the time axis unchanged.

Sum · below the horizon

C₁ + C₂—
C₃ (whole)—
C₁ : C₂ : C₃ = BSP² : SPD² : BD²—
The altitude from SP cuts the right triangle into two triangles similar to it. Areas of similar figures go as the squares of their sides, so the parts summing to the whole is a² + b² = c². The form is part plus part.

What the sheet shows

The blue figure is a sum: two parts fill the whole, C₁ + C₂ = C₃. The pink figure is a difference: the whole minus a part is the remaining part, A₂ − A₁ = A₃, which is what the overlap cancellation produces. One has the shape of a² + b² = c²; the other has the shape of T² − D² = I². Both are built with the same affine moves on the same sheet. Drag the shear slider and every identity holds while every right angle disappears.

One line crosses the horizon. The pink rectangle is built on the station point: its top edge is as far above the horizon as SP is below. So the triangle P–T–D above is the reflection of P–SP–D below, and A₃ = C₂ for every position of P. That is an affine fact, two triangles on one base with apexes equidistant from the horizon, and it is what lets the difference figure and the sum figure share a piece.

So the additive and the subtractive forms are both available in the same plane, from the same square, before any absolute is chosen. What the absolute later supplies is which lengths the areas stand for. The circle makes C₁, C₂, C₃ the squares on the sides of a Euclidean right triangle. Light lines, if the slide direction were set along them, would make A₁, A₂, A₃ the light rectangles on the sides of a Minkowski one. The figure as drawn commits to neither; it shows the two forms and stops.

The frame underneath both

The green construction places four points on the horizon. SP sees B and D at a right angle because it is on the circle; it also sees C′ and A′, the ends of the vertical diameter, at a right angle, for the same reason. Two perpendicular diameters give inscribed angles of 45° between the pairs, and a pencil with that property is harmonic: (A, C; B, D) = −1, or in lengths from M, A · C = r². The same four sight lines cut the time axis in A′, B′, C′, D′, and the cross-ratio there is −1 as well, since a perspectivity preserves it. This is the one thing on the sheet that survives even when the areas are gone: shear the figure and the sum, the difference and the harmonic set all hold; project it from a new center and only the harmonic set is left.
