The fold line as axis of revolution

One line, two uses: the hinge of a mirror, and the hinge of a drawing. Plate v1. Revised 11 November 2026: placed in the thread as a side plate (line below). No other change.

The mirror thread · side plate B · follows side plate A, Dimension: the fold · index

Two fold lines appear in the plan-and-elevation sketch of a pin being turned out of its plane. One is the hinge of the half-turn that produces the mirror; its angle is free, and its angle is the measure. The other is the line between plan and elevation; its angle is fixed at 90° by the convention of descriptive geometry, and it is never read. This plate works out what each one does to the figure, in the same drawing, driven by the same dial.

Terms used on this plate

fixed line (of a map)
the line of points the map leaves where they are. A mirror of the plane is determined by its fixed line; a half-turn of space is determined by its fixed line (its axis).
fold line
on this plate, always the mirror’s hinge: the fixed line shared by the mirror of the plane and the half-turn of space. Drawn teal.
hinge a
the line in which the plan plane and the elevation plane meet; the drafting hinge, about which the elevation plane is laid down onto the plan. Named a to match the Monge plates, where it is the line H∩V. Drawn dark grey. Not called ground line: Monge’s ligne de terre belongs to first-angle projection, where the object stands on the plan plane; these plates are plan-over-elevation, where that plane lies above the object.
revolution (Monge: rabattement)
rotating a figure about a line lying in a picture plane. Each point travels on a circle whose plane is perpendicular to the axis.
trace
on this plate: what the plan sees of a figure in space, its orthogonal projection onto the plan plane. (On the Monge plates the same word names the line where two picture planes meet; that line is called hinge a here, so the two uses never share a plate.)
trace map
the map of the plan plane that sends each point’s position at 0° to the trace of its position at the current angle θ. Written Tθ.
congruence
a map of the plane that preserves every distance. Its determinant is +1 or −1 and nothing else.
projector
a line joining a point’s plan position to its elevation position, perpendicular to the hinge a. Drawn green, dashed.

1. The pin, revolved — plan over elevation

The pin lies in the plan plane, parallel to the fold line, at distance d from it. Turn the dial: the pin revolves about the fold line. The elevation looks along the fold line, so the fold line is a point there and the pin is a point travelling on a circle of radius d. The plan sees only the pin’s shadow, which slides toward the fold line, crosses it, and arrives on the far side.

The projectors are the plan traces of the circles of revolution: each circle’s plane is perpendicular to the fold line, so the plan sees it edge-on, as a line perpendicular to the fold line. That is Monge’s construction for rabattement, and it is also why the pin’s shadow slides along a straight line.

2. What the plan sees: the trace map

Take the pin’s side of the plan plane as a figure in its own right — here a flag, so that handedness shows. Revolve the whole figure with the pin. The plan sees each point at distance x from the fold line arrive at distance x·cos θ. The trace map is a compression toward the fold line:

Tθ(x, y) = (x·cos θ, y), determinant cos θ.

The plan’s own congruences have determinant +1 or −1 and nothing between. The trace map runs continuously from +1 to −1, which a congruence cannot do, because it passes through every value between — including 0, where the figure is crushed onto the fold line and the map is not invertible. This is the exact content of “the sign would have to jump”: in the plane, the only way from identity to mirror is to pass through a collapse. Space replaces shrinking by turning.

3. The hinge a: the same construction, used once

Descriptive geometry also revolves about a line in a picture plane — but it revolves the elevation plane itself, and only ever by 90°. In working position the elevation plane stands perpendicular to the plan, so each plane is edge-on in the other: depth in one is height in the other. To draw both on one sheet, the elevation plane is laid down about the hinge a. Look along a to watch it happen.

angle between the planes90° — working position: each plane is edge-on in the other

Only the two ends of this dial are ever used: 90° is where the elevation plane does its work, 0° is where it is drawn. The intermediate angles are never read, so the hinge a carries a view and no measure. In the pin figure, 90° was the angle of collapse; here the same angle is chosen on purpose — the elevation plane is placed exactly where the plan would see it as a line, then swung flat.

So the mirror fold line is not a new object. It is the axis of a rabattement, the thing descriptive geometry has always had — with its dial unlocked, and read.
