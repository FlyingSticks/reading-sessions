The fold

Squaring and its two roots, in five points. Version 3, 31 October 2026.

Six diagrams, one map. The map is squaring, f ↦ f2, on the plane. Diagram 1 shows what squaring does to four points. Diagram 2 shows the three planes that a single question — what squares to −1, to 0, to +1? — generates, each with its unit locus and its null locus. Diagram 3 shows squaring and the principal root as mirror images under the one-to-one / onto distinction. Diagram 4 shows how restricting to one half of the plane repairs both defects at once. Diagram 5 shows the seam: the edge of that half, where the root is forced to jump. Diagram 6 shows the three generators of diagram 2 as motions of their planes, so that "squares to" reads as "done twice gives". A glossary of every term used follows; drawing words and mathematical words are kept apart.

1. Four points fold to two

The same thing happens everywhere in the plane, not only at these four points: f and −f always have the same square. The two points of each pair are a half-turn about 0 apart. So the whole picture is: turn the plane by a half-turn about 0, so that every point lies on top of its opposite, then count the two stacked points as one. That is the fold.

2. One question, three planes

Two things to notice. First, the table's answers are pairs: ±i, ±ε, ±j. The fold is already present in the table, and writing "f = i" would be choosing a reading without saying so. Second, each column's caveat (ε ≠ 0, j ≠ ±1) is what makes its answer a new generator rather than an old number; without the caveat the first and third columns collapse to f = 0 and f = ±1.

The null row is where the three planes differ most. The Euclidean form vanishes only at 0; the parabolic form vanishes along a whole line (counted twice, because x² has a double root at 0); the Minkowski form vanishes along two lines, which are also the asymptotes of its unit hyperbola.

3. Squaring and the root are mirror images

The two defects are the same defect seen from either end of the arrows. Squaring: two arrows in, one target — surplus on the input side. Root: one arrow out, two candidate targets, one dropped — deficit on the output side. One congruence (the square), two readings (the two roots).

A point of bookkeeping that matters: "onto" is judged against a declared target. The left panel declares three targets and the right panel declares five, because the mirror swaps input set and target set. Change the declared target and the verdict changes — which is what the next diagram exploits.

4. Choosing a half repairs both defects

This is the cleanest statement of the fold: the whole plane is in one-to-one and onto correspondence with either half of itself, by squaring. Choosing a half undoes the fold. The set-aside half is the other reading; it would serve exactly as well, and its squares are the same squares.

5. The seam: the half has an edge

This is the fold stated as a fact about turning. Squaring doubles the angle, so the root halves it. Carry w once around 0 and its angle gains 360°; the root's angle gains only 180°, which lands it on the opposite root. The half chosen in diagram 4 therefore cannot be chosen smoothly: somewhere there is an edge where the root passes from one reading to the other, and that edge is the seam. The principal rule puts the seam on the negative real axis; any other rule puts it on some other ray out of 0, but every rule has one.

6. The three generators as motions

This is the sense in which a symbol can square to 0 without being 0, or to +1 without being ±1. The symbol is not a point on the real line; it is a motion of the plane, and its square is the motion done twice. Two quarter-turns are a half-turn, which is ×(−1). Two collapses leave nothing, which is ×0 — yet one collapse is not nothing, so ε ≠ 0. Two reflections are the identity, which is ×(+1) — yet one reflection is neither the identity nor a half-turn, so j ≠ ±1. On the real line the number 1 has only two square roots; in the j-plane it has four, ±1 and ±j, because a reflection is now available as a motion.

The algebra behind the picture is one line: (x + yg)(u + vg) = xu + g²·yv + (xv + yu)g. Everything in it is ordinary except g², and g² is a decree. The three decrees −1, 0, +1 give the three planes.

The null loci of diagram 2 reappear here as the numbers with no reciprocal. In the ε-plane, (a + bε)(c + dε) = ac + (ad + bc)ε, so reaching 1 needs ac = 1, impossible when a = 0: the line x = 0 has no reciprocals, and nothing undoes a collapse. In the j-plane, (1 + j)(1 − j) = 1 − j² = 0: two nonzero numbers with product zero, so neither has a reciprocal, and the same holds all along the lines x = ±y. Only the Euclidean plane has a reciprocal for everything but 0 — one reason it is the plane that became "the" complex numbers.

Glossary

Drawing words and mathematical words are kept apart. Where a word has both senses, both are given. Terms that clash with a standard usage say so.

map
A rule assigning to each input exactly one output. Squaring is a map. "Take the square root" is not a map until a rule picks one root (see principal root).
arrow (drawing word)
In diagrams 3 and 4, a drawn line with a head, standing for one input–output pair of a map. It is not a vector and has no length or direction of its own. A line with heads at both ends stands for a pair that works in both directions.
dot (drawing word)
A small drawn circle standing for a point. Filled dots are points in play; dashed dots are points the map never reaches, or points set aside.
declared target
The set a map is said to land in, fixed in advance. Whether a map is onto depends on it. Diagram 3 declares three targets for squaring and five for the root; diagram 4 declares a half.
one-to-one (injective)
No two inputs share an output: no two arrows arrive at the same dot. Squaring on the whole plane fails this, since (+1)² = (−1)². The principal root passes it.
onto (surjective)
Every point of the declared target is some input's output: every target dot has at least one arrow arriving. Squaring passes this, since every complex number has a square root. The principal root, with the whole plane declared as target, fails it.
bijection
A map that is both one-to-one and onto. It can be run backwards without ambiguity. Diagram 4 shows one.
restriction
Keeping a map's rule but shrinking its allowed inputs (or its declared target). Diagram 4 restricts squaring's inputs to one half and the root's target to that half.
two-to-one
A map under which every output, with possibly a few exceptions, has exactly two inputs. Squaring is two-to-one with one exception, 0.
fold
A picture for a two-to-one map. Turn the plane by a half-turn about 0, so that each point lies on its opposite, then count the two stacked points as one. Not a crease along a line.
half-turn about 0
The rotation by 180° about the point 0; it sends f to −f. Applying it twice gives the identity, so it is an involution. It exchanges the two roots of every square.
half (of the plane)
A set of inputs containing exactly one of f, −f for every f ≠ 0, together with 0. The principal half is: positive real part, plus the positive imaginary axis. Other halves exist; each is a reading.
principal root
The square root chosen by a fixed rule, so that "take the root" becomes a map. The usual rule: the root with positive real part, and for negative reals the root on the positive imaginary axis. Any other consistent rule reaches a different half of the plane, but always exactly a half.
seam
The edge of a half: the ray out of 0 on which the principal root jumps from one reading to the other. For the principal rule it is the negative real axis. Every rule has a seam somewhere; none has none. (Standard name: branch cut.)
sign
The choice between f and −f. Squaring forgets the sign; taking a root has to be told it.
angle doubling
Squaring doubles a point's angle about 0 and squares its distance from 0. The root halves the angle. Once around for the square is half around for the root; this is why a seam is forced.
generator
A symbol g whose square is −1, 0 or +1 but which is not itself an ordinary real number. There are three: i (square −1), ε (square 0, ε ≠ 0) and j (square +1, j ≠ ±1). Each generates a plane of numbers x + y·g. Not called "unit" here: in algebra a unit is an invertible element, and ε is not invertible.
motion (as used in diagram 6)
What multiplying every point of a plane by a fixed number does to the plane. Multiplying by the generator gives: a quarter-turn (Euclidean), a collapse onto the ε-axis (parabolic), a reflection in the diagonal (Minkowski). "Reflection" here is the drawing sense — mirror across a line — and the line is the diagonal x = y.
collapse
The motion ×ε: (x, y) ↦ (0, x). The y-coordinate is dropped and the x-coordinate is moved onto the ε-axis. One collapse leaves a line's worth of points; a second leaves only 0.
invertible, reciprocal
A number is invertible if some number multiplies it to 1; that number is its reciprocal. On the real line and in the Euclidean plane everything but 0 is invertible. In the parabolic plane the line x = 0 is not; in the Minkowski plane the lines x = ±y are not. In each plane the non-invertible numbers are exactly the null locus.
form
Of a plane with generator g: the quantity x² − g²·y², the square of a number's size. Euclidean: x² + y². Parabolic: x². Minkowski: x² − y².
unit locus
The curve where the form equals 1. Circle; two parallel lines x = ±1; hyperbola (with its conjugate, where the form equals −1).
null locus
The set where the form equals 0. The point 0; the line x = 0 counted twice; two crossing lines. Not called "absolute" here: in Cayley–Klein usage the absolute is the conic at infinity of a plane, and the two words should not be confused.
Euclidean, parabolic, Minkowski
The three planes of diagram 2, named after the geometry their generator produces. Parabolic is also called Galilean or dual; Minkowski is also called hyperbolic or split-complex.
congruence, reading
As used in the phrase "one congruence, two readings": the square is the one value; the two roots are the two readings of it. This is the sense used on this page only; the book's own definitions govern elsewhere.
