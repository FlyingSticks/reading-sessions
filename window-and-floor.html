Window and floor

The plane comes last, or the plane comes first — and why that decides what is locked and what is free

This page is the walk-through of one afternoon's leap, kept on human terms. It has two live instruments. The first shows one plane doing two jobs. The second puts the book's rate square under your finger and lets you watch the seam appear. The text between them says only what the instruments show; the theorems live in Chapter 2 and Chapter 3.

One plane, two jobs

A camera treats the picture plane as a window. A point sits out in the world; a sight line runs from it to the eye; the mark is wherever that line happens to pierce the glass. The mark is an output. It is the last thing made, and the camera cannot put it anywhere else.

A glide draftsman treats the same plane as a floor. A footprint is set at level zero; a straight depth line is raised from it; depth is laid off along the line with a ruler. The mark is an input. The floor is the first thing made, and the ruler is the draftsman's to choose.

Slide the probe. On the left, watch the window's mark move because the ray moved. On the right, the footprint never moves; only the tick along the drawn line moves, and it moves by whichever ruler is chosen.

The hollow purple ring on the sheet is where the window's mark would fall. Under Turn the teal tick sits on it at every depth: this rig is on the seam and welded. Under Reach the tick walks off the sheet as z grows, because equal steps leave infinity unmarked — Theorem 0.

What is locked and what is free

Here is the sentence the afternoon produced, checked against Chapter 2 and holding:

The window has a free law and a welded gauge. The floor has a fixed law and a free gauge.

A law is the shape of the machinery — which lines the marks run along, and how fast each channel contracts. A gauge is the ruler laid along those lines. The camera chooses its law by placing its slits, and once they are placed, optics fixes where every depth lands: the gauge is welded. The draftsman's law is fixed in advance — straight lines from footprints — and the ruler along them is free: equal steps (Reach) or harmonic crowding toward a marked vanishing point (Turn). So the freedom you felt on the glide side did not vanish on the projective side. It moved up a level, from the ruler to the law.

Theorem 0 says why the two rulers cannot be one ruler. A scale on a straight line cannot both mark equal steps and give infinity a finite mark. Reach keeps the steps and lets infinity go; Turn marks infinity and pays in crowding. You saw both above.

The rate square

The book draws every rig as a point (ru, rv): the rate at which each channel contracts toward its seat. That square is the camera's law-space — the two numbers the slits are free to set. The draftsman has no point on it, because the draftsman's law is not a pair of rates but a class: straight. The question the square answers is therefore not "free or welded" but "do the two laws agree?"

Drag the point. The purple curve is the camera's image of one depth line; the teal line is the draftsman's. Purple dots are the camera's marks at equal depth steps 1 to 5; teal dots are the draftsman's under the chosen ruler.

Convention as in Chapter 2: camera C(λ) = (x₀/(1+ruλ), y₀/(1+rvλ)); glide G(μ) = F + μd with d the camera's starting direction. Both rulers are calibrated to agree at level 1. Turn takes μ = λ/(1+rλ), r the welded channel's rate; Reach takes μ = λ/(1+r), equal steps.

The seam is two steps, not one

The afternoon's first phrasing had the seam as the place where the draftsman's free choice equals what the camera forced. That was one step too few, and the instrument shows the missing one.

Step one is a fact about laws. Eliminate λ from the camera's curve and you get a straight line only when ru = rv or one rate is zero: the diagonal and the two edges, exactly the gold lines. On the seam the camera's law happens to fall inside the draftsman's class. Off it, the camera's line bows, and nothing done with a ruler can straighten a curve.

Step two is a turn of the free gauge. Sit on the seam with Reach and the marks still disagree — the teal dots are evenly spaced, the purple ones crowd. Turn the ruler to Turn, with the vanishing point where the camera's is, and the dots land on each other: the weld. Only then is the drawing the photograph.

Two things worth holding. The seam is a condition on the camera's law, because the camera is the one with a law to choose. And the weld is an act of the draftsman, because the draftsman is the one with a gauge to turn. Each reading contributes the freedom it has.

Datum both ways

Whatever comes first in a construction is what things are measured from. Whatever comes last is what things are measured at. The floor is the datum of depth because it is laid first, level zero of the slab. The window is the datum of scale because it is reached last, the one level where magnification is exactly 1. At z = 0 both rulers read 1 separately, which is what qualifies the plane to be the exempt reference for both readings at once. This paragraph is seasoning, not book text; it is here so the phrasing can be compared with itself in a month.

TheoremThe camera's image of a depth line is straight exactly on the diagonal and the two zero edges. Algebra: y = y₀·rux / ((ru − rv)x + rvx₀), linear iff ru = rv, ru = 0 or rv = 0. Two thousand random rigs, no exception.
Re-verifiedThe weld μ = λ/(1+rλ) puts the camera's marks on the glide's to machine precision on the seam (residual 2·10⁻¹⁶); the camera's infinity lands at the finite mark 1/r, which is what makes the welded gauge Turn and not Reach.
ObservationOrder of construction: the window is the last thing made, so its mark is an output; the floor is the first, so its mark is an input. "Free law, welded gauge; fixed law, free gauge" is a reading of Chapter 2 §2.2, not new content.
