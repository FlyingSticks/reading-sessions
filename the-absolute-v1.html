The absolute

How a decree chooses the centre. Three planes; mirror lines through 0. Version 1, 11 November 2026.

The mirror thread · plate 11 of 11 · previous: The root · index

The involution plate found that the mirror with line m is a harmonic pairing whose centre K is at infinity, and that the one job of a decree is to say in which direction K lies. It left open how the decree says so. The three-pairs plate gave a rule that uses the unit locus: take the tangent where m crosses it. This plate gives the rule with no unit locus at all. A decree is a pairing of directions. The direction of K is the partner of the direction of m.

Figure 1 shows the pairing for one line m at a time. Figure 2 shows many pairs at once, and the three kinds of pairing that the three decrees give. Section 3 names what the pairing leaves fixed: the absolute. A list of what is claimed, and a glossary, follow. Drawing words and mathematical words are kept apart.

1. Directions, read on a horizon

A point at infinity is a direction (the involution plate). A direction cannot be given a dot on the page, but it can be read off. Draw the line through 0 in that direction, and see where it crosses the horizontal line one unit above 0. This plate calls that horizontal line the horizon, and the place where a line through 0 crosses it the reading of that direction. The reading is a single ordinary number, t: how far along the horizon the crossing is, measured from the point directly above 0. For any point x + g y of the line other than 0, t = x ÷ y.

One direction has no reading: the 1-axis itself, which runs parallel to the horizon and never crosses it. The ε-axis, straight up from 0, has the reading 0.

The word is the draftsman's. In a perspective drawing the horizon carries the vanishing points, one for each direction on the ground, and each is found by a line from the station point. Here 0 plays the part of the station point. The “Two Absolutes” page uses the same arrangement.

The rule

The three-pairs plate says: if the mirror line m is the line of the number u, its across direction is the line of g × u. Put u = x + g y. Then g × u = g² y + g x: the point that is g² y across and x up. Its reading is g² y ÷ x, and the reading of u is t = x ÷ y. So:

t × t′ = g²

where t is the reading of m and t′ is the reading of its across direction. That is the whole rule. The decree enters as the single number on the right, and the unit locus is not used.

In Figure 1 the slider turns m about 0. The point T on the horizon is the reading of m. The point T′ is the reading of the dotted line, which is drawn by the three-pairs rule, not by the rule above. The ledger then multiplies the two readings as they stand on the drawing.

direction of m

The earlier plates draw only Minkowski mirror lines flatter than the diagonal. Here the slider carries m through every direction, steeper ones included, because the pairing treats a line and its across direction alike: if T′ is the partner of T, then T is the partner of T′.

2. Three kinds of pairing

Figure 2 shows several pairs at once. Each arc joins a reading t to its partner t′. The arcs are a drawing device only: an arc is there to show which two points belong together.

  • Euclidean, t × t′ = −1. The product is negative, so the two points of a pair lie on opposite sides of the point above 0. Every arc spans that point, and any two arcs cross: any two pairs interlock.
  • Minkowski, t × t′ = +1. The product is positive, so the two points of a pair lie on the same side. The arcs nest, and no two cross. Each nest closes down on one point: +1 on the right, −1 on the left.
  • Parabolic, t × t′ = 0. Every reading has the same partner, 0. All the arcs end at one point. This is not a pairing in the full sense, since 0 has no single partner of its own; it is what the other two kinds become between them, as g² passes through 0.

The sign of g² is therefore visible on the horizon with no arithmetic: arcs that cross, arcs that nest, arcs that meet at a point. The standard names are the elliptic, hyperbolic and parabolic cases, and the parabolic plane carries the third name. The “Two Absolutes” page shows the first two, as a negative and a positive product on its horizon.

3. The absolute

A direction is self-paired when it is its own partner: t′ = t. By the rule, that is t × t = g². So the self-paired readings are the square roots of g², taken among ordinary numbers.

PlaneEquationSelf-paired directions
Euclideant² = −1None on the page. No ordinary number has the square −1.
Parabolict² = 0One, counted twice: the reading 0, the ε-axis.
Minkowskit² = +1Two: the readings +1 and −1, the two diagonals.

These are the directions of the null locus of the fold page, where the form is 0: a point of the line with reading t is t y + g y, its form is (t² − g²) × y², and that is 0 exactly when t² = g². The self-paired directions of a plane, taken together, are its absolute. Four things already met on the plates turn out to be this one thing.

  • The lines that cannot be mirror lines (three-pairs plate). If m is self-paired, K lies in the direction of m itself. A harmonic pairing needs its centre off its line. So a self-paired line has no mirror.
  • The null locus (fold page), as just shown.
  • Where the unit locus leaves the page (root plate, section 4). The two pieces of the parabolic and Minkowski unit loci run off to infinity in the self-paired directions. The Euclidean plane has none, and its unit locus never leaves the page: it is the closed loop on which the two roots are joined.
  • The count of square roots of g² among ordinary numbers: none, one counted twice, two. This is the fold page's question, asked of directions.

In the Minkowski plane the pairing is harmonic. The partner T′ of T is the point for which the two self-paired points, T and T′ are harmonic: their cross-ratio is −1. The ledger of Figure 1 measures it. So in that plane “across” means “harmonic with the two diagonals”, and the mirror is built from −1 twice over: once to find K, and once, on the involution plate, to find each partner.

In the Euclidean plane the same sentence holds, with the two square roots of −1 in place of +1 and −1. Those roots are the i and −i of the fold page. They are not ordinary numbers and have no place on the horizon, so this is stated here and not drawn. What can be drawn is its consequence, the right angle at 0 in Figure 2.

What this hands on. The measure of a pair has so far been read from the unit locus, as twice a sector area. It can also be read on the horizon, from the cross-ratio of the two lines with the two self-paired directions. That is the next plate.

6. Glossary

Mathematical words

Direction
What all lines parallel to one line have in common. Each direction is one point at infinity (involution plate). Through 0 there is exactly one line in each direction.
Reading, t
The number that names a direction on this plate: for any point x + g y of the line through 0 in that direction, other than 0, t = x ÷ y. The 1-axis has no reading.
Pairing of directions
The rule that gives each direction a partner. On this plate: t × t′ = g². The partner of the direction of a mirror line is its across direction.
Self-paired
A direction that is its own partner.
Absolute
The self-paired directions of a plane, taken together. The word is Cayley's and Klein's: the figure at infinity against which a geometry measures. For these three planes it comes down to this: no direction (Euclidean), one counted twice (parabolic), two (Minkowski). The fold page kept the word apart from “null locus”; the two are now seen to be the same lines, the null locus being the lines through 0 and the absolute their directions.
Interlock
Two pairs of points on a line interlock when exactly one point of the second pair lies between the two points of the first. Standard word: the pairs separate each other.
Harmonic, cross-ratio
As on the involution plate.
Across direction, unit locus, decree, form, null locus
As on the three-pairs plate and the fold page.
Centre K
As on the involution plate: the centre of the harmonic pairing that is the mirror with line m. It is the point at infinity in the across direction of m.

Drawing words

Horizon
The horizontal line one unit above 0, on which directions are read. It stands in for the line at infinity, which cannot be drawn. A draftsman's word, used as on the “Two Absolutes” page.
T, T′
The points of the horizon where m and its across direction cross it.
Arc
In Figure 2, a half-circle joining a reading to its partner. It shows which two points form a pair and has no other meaning.
Filled dot, ringed dot
As on the earlier plates: a point given, a point found. “Ringed” is a drawing word only.
Dashed line in the plane's colour
A self-paired direction, drawn as the line through 0.
