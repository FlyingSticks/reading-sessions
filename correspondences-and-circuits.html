Correspondences and Circuits

What an involution is made of, one requirement at a time, and the patterns a reversible correspondence can have.

What the patterns say

  • On finitely many things, an involution is a reversible correspondence with no circuit longer than 2.
  • The identity is the one with no circuit longer than 1. It counts as an involution: the one that does nothing.
  • On finitely many things, "run backwards, it is a different rule" holds exactly when some circuit has length 3 or more.
  • For infinitely many things, add "and no endless chain" to the first, and "or there is an endless chain" to the third (figure 3).

Reading figure 3: "sent to" and "reached by"

Both phrases describe one arrow. "n is sent to m": the arrow's tail is at n and its head is at m. "m is reached by n": the same arrow, described from the head end.

The endless chain. Every arrow in that row points to the right. Take the dot −1. Forward question, "where is −1 sent?": to 0, since −1 + 1 = 0. Then 0 is sent to 1, and so on, without end. Backward question, "which number is sent to −1?": −2, since −2 + 1 = −1. And −2 is reached by −3, and so on, without end. The same holds beyond the drawn dots: −3 is sent to −2, and −3 is reached by −4. "Endless in both directions" means only this: there is no last dot going forward and no first dot going backward. No arrow points to the left.

The chain with a beginning. Ask the backward question of 1: which counting number is sent to 1? It would have to be 0, and 0 is not in the collection. So no arrowhead lands on 1. That is the whole content of "1 is reached by nothing": an arrow leaves 1 (to 2), and no arrow arrives at 1. The red ring marks that dot.

Why that breaks reversibility. Reversing means turning every arrow around, so the rule becomes "subtract 1". On all integers that works everywhere. On the counting numbers it fails at 1: 1 − 1 = 0 is not in the collection, so the reversed rule has nowhere to send 1.

Terms

The plain words are the plate's own. The standard name, for looking things up elsewhere, follows each in grey.

correspondence
A rule sending each thing of a collection to some thing of the same collection. Standard: a map, or function, from a set to itself.
sent to
"n is sent to m": the arrow's tail is at n and its head is at m. Standard: m is the image of n.
reached by
"m is reached by n": the same arrow, described from the head end. "Reached by nothing": no arrowhead lands there. Standard: n is a preimage of m; reached by nothing means not in the image.
reversible
Every thing is reached exactly once, so the rule can be run backwards. Standard: a bijection; for finitely many things, a permutation.
no two arrive at the same place
One of the two checks for infinitely many things. Standard: one-to-one, or injective.
every one is reached
The other check. Standard: onto, or surjective.
circuit
A closed chain of arrows: follow them and return to the start. Its length is the number of dots on it. Standard: a cycle.
pair
A circuit of length 2. Standard: a 2-cycle. A reversible correspondence that is one 2-cycle and nothing else is called a transposition.
pattern
How many circuits of each length a reversible correspondence on finitely many things has, setting aside which thing is which. Standard: its cycle type.
endless chain
A chain of arrows with no beginning and no end, visiting nothing twice. Standard: a two-way infinite orbit of a bijection.
chain with a beginning
A chain of arrows starting at a thing reached by nothing.
involution
A reversible correspondence whose backward rule is its forward rule. Equivalently: no circuit longer than 2, and no endless chain. The identity is included. Standard: involution.
identity
The correspondence sending each thing to itself. It is an involution, the one that does nothing. Standard: identity.
