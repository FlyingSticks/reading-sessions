The measure

Read on the horizon as a cross-ratio. Three planes; lines through 0. Version 1, 11 November 2026.

The mirror thread · plate 12 of 12 · previous: The absolute · index

The measure of a pair of lines has been read, since the three-pairs plate, from the unit locus: twice the area of the sector between the two unit points. The absolute plate read a decree on a horizon instead, and found the mirror there with no unit locus. This plate does the same for the measure. On the horizon there are four points to hand: the readings of the two lines, and the two self-paired readings. Four points on a line have a cross-ratio. That cross-ratio holds the measure.

One figure serves all three planes. Section 1 states the result for each plane. Section 2 gives the reasons. Section 3 sets the cross-ratio beside the number q² of the root plate and finds them to be one thing. Section 4 returns to the value −1.

1. The result, plane by plane

Let A and B be two lines through 0, with readings tA and tB on the horizon. Let the self-paired readings of the plane be +γ and −γ: the two square roots of g² (absolute plate, section 3). Write R for the cross-ratio of the four readings taken in the order tA, tB, +γ, −γ. By the formula of the involution plate,

R = ((tA − γ) × (tB + γ)) ÷ ((tA + γ) × (tB − γ)).

In the Euclidean plane a line has two unit points, b and −b, and the horizon cannot tell them apart: it holds lines, not unit points. The sector is therefore drawn to whichever unit point of B is nearer to a, and the measure runs from −90° to 90°. This is the fold of the root plate once more: the two roots q and −q have the same square.

2. The reasons

The Euclidean plane

Take the unit point of A: a = x + i y, with a × ā = 1. The reading of A is t = x ÷ y. Then

(t − i) ÷ (t + i) = (x − i y) ÷ (x + i y) = ā ÷ a = ā².

The last step uses 1 ÷ a = ā. The same for B gives (tB + i) ÷ (tB − i) = b². Multiply the two:

R = ā² × b² = (b × ā)² = q².

So in the Euclidean plane the cross-ratio on the horizon is the number q² of the root plate, exactly. Its angle is twice the angle from A to B, which is the doubling. Nothing is assumed.

The Minkowski plane

Take the unit point of A: a = x + j y, with x² − y² = 1, that is, (x − y) × (x + y) = 1. Write λ(a) for the ordinary number x + y. Then x − y = 1 ÷ λ(a), and

(t − 1) ÷ (t + 1) = (x − y) ÷ (x + y) = 1 ÷ λ(a)².

The same for B gives (tB + 1) ÷ (tB − 1) = λ(b)². So R = λ(b)² ÷ λ(a)².

Two facts about λ finish it. First, λ turns the plane's multiplication into ordinary multiplication: λ(a × b) = λ(a) × λ(b), which is one line of algebra with j² = 1; and λ(ā) = 1 ÷ λ(a) for a unit point. Hence

R = λ(b × ā)² = λ(q)² = λ(q²).

Second, the measure of a unit point is the logarithm of its λ. (This is the standard fact that the area cut off under a hyperbola is a logarithm. It is used here and not proved; the ledger checks it against the drawn sector.) The measure of q² is twice the measure from A to B. So the measure from A to B is half the logarithm of R.

The parabolic plane

The unit point of A is a = 1 + g s, where s is the slope of A, and its reading is t = 1 ÷ s. The three-pairs plate found the measure from A to B to be the difference of the slopes. That is 1 ÷ tB − 1 ÷ tA.

The cross-ratio itself is no help: with both self-paired readings at 0, the formula gives R = (tA × tB) ÷ (tA × tB) = 1, whatever the lines. But pull the two self-paired readings a little apart, to +γ and −γ. Then R is a little more or less than 1, and (R − 1) ÷ (2γ) comes closer and closer to the difference of slopes as γ shrinks. The ledger shows it with γ = 0.001. So the parabolic measure is the last trace of the cross-ratio, caught as the two points of the absolute close into one.

Measures add because cross-ratios multiply

For three lines A, B, C, the formula for R gives at once R(A, C) = R(A, B) × R(B, C): the middle factors cancel. A logarithm turns a product into a sum, and angles of points on the unit circle add when the points are multiplied. So the measure from A to C is the measure from A to B plus the measure from B to C, in every plane.

3. One object, seen twice

The root plate attached a number to a pair of lines: q², with q = b ÷ a. The congruence “A then B” is multiplication by it. This plate attaches a cross-ratio to the same pair. Section 2 shows how the two are related.

Minkowskix + j yx + y
Parabolic1 + g × (twice the measure)1

One sentence covers the three rows: R is q² with g given the value of a self-paired reading. In the Euclidean plane that value is i, which is g already, so nothing changes. In the Minkowski plane it is +1, and x + j y becomes x + y. In the parabolic plane it is 0, and the part of q² that carried the measure is wiped out, which is why that plane needs the limit of section 2.

So the unit locus and the horizon are two ways of holding the same pair of lines. The first holds it as a point one can multiply. The second holds it as four points on a line and the one number that four such points have.

4. The value −1, again

The absolute plate found that B is the across direction of A exactly when the four readings are harmonic: R = −1. Read that with the table of section 1.

  • Euclidean. −1 is the point of the unit circle half-way round, at 180°. Half of that is 90°: A and B are perpendicular. And q² = −1 is the half-turn. This is row 2 of the dictionary on the third-mirror plate: two lines are perpendicular exactly when “A then B” is an involution.
  • Minkowski. −1 is a negative number and has no logarithm. There is no measure from a line to its across direction: one is flatter than the diagonals and the other steeper, and no turning of the plane carries the first to the second. R is negative for every such pair. The sign of R says whether a measure exists; its size says what the measure is.
  • Parabolic. R is always 1 and never −1. No two parabolic mirror lines are perpendicular, as the third-mirror plate records.

So the one value that incidence can state, −1, is the one value at which a measure is either a right angle or absent. Every other value of R is a measure, and to read it a decree is needed first: it supplies γ.

6. Glossary

Mathematical words

Reading, horizon, self-paired, absolute
As on the absolute plate.
γ
A self-paired reading: a square root of g² among the numbers at hand. Minkowski: 1 (the other is −1). Euclidean: i (the other is −i); these are not ordinary numbers and are not on the page. Parabolic: 0, counted twice.
R
The cross-ratio of the four readings tA, tB, +γ, −γ, in that order, by the formula of the involution plate.
Measure from A to B
As on the three-pairs plate: twice the area of the sector from the unit point of A to the unit point of B, positive counter-clockwise. Euclidean: the angle, shown here in degrees. Parabolic: the difference of slopes. Minkowski: a plain number.
Logarithm (written ln)
The rule that turns multiplication into addition: ln(u × v) = ln u + ln v, with ln 1 = 0. Only positive ordinary numbers have one.
λ
For a Minkowski number x + j y: the ordinary number x + y. It is the number with j given the value +1. This plate's own letter.
q, q², unit point, bar
As on the root plate.
Angle of a point of the unit circle
Its measure in the sense of the root plate: the angle from 1 round to the point, counter-clockwise.
Flatter, steeper (than the diagonals)
A line through 0 is flatter when it lies between the diagonals on the side of the 1-axis, steeper when it lies on the side of the j-axis. Flatter lines cross the unit locus; steeper lines do not.

Drawing words

TA, TB
The points of the horizon where A and B cross it.
Sector
As on the three-pairs plate: the shaded region bounded by two segments from 0 and the piece of the unit locus between them. In the parabolic plane it is a triangle.
Filled dot, ringed dot, dashed line
As on the absolute plate.
