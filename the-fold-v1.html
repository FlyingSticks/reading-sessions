The fold

Squaring and its two roots, in five points. 30 October 2026.

Three diagrams, one map. The map is squaring, f ↦ f2, on the plane. The first diagram shows what squaring does to four points. The second shows the three planes that a single question — what squares to −1, to 0, to +1? — generates. The third shows squaring and the square root as mirror images under the one-to-one / onto distinction. A glossary of every term used follows.

1. Four points fold to two

The same thing happens everywhere in the plane, not only at these four points: f and −f always have the same square. The two points of each pair are a half-turn about 0 apart. So the whole picture is: turn the plane by a half-turn about 0, so that every point lies on top of its opposite, then count the two stacked points as one. That is the fold.

Glossary

Drawing words and mathematical words are kept apart. Where a word has both a drawing sense and a mathematical sense, both are given.

map
A rule assigning to each input exactly one output. Squaring is a map. "Take the square root" is not a map until a sign is chosen (see principal root).
arrow (drawing word)
In diagram 3, a drawn line with a head, standing for one input–output pair of a map. It is not a vector and has no length or direction of its own.
dot (drawing word)
A small drawn circle standing for a point. Filled dots are points in play; dashed dots are points the map never reaches.
one-to-one (injective)
A map is one-to-one when no two inputs share an output: no two arrows arrive at the same dot. Squaring fails this, since (+1)² = (−1)². The principal root passes it.
onto (surjective)
A map is onto when every point of the target is some input's output: every target dot has at least one arrow arriving. Squaring passes this, since every complex number has a square root. The principal root fails it, reaching only half the plane.
two-to-one
A map under which every output (with possibly a few exceptions) has exactly two inputs. Squaring is two-to-one with one exception, 0.
fold
A picture for a two-to-one map. Turn the plane by a half-turn about 0, so that each point lies on its opposite, then count the two stacked points as one. Not a crease along a line.
half-turn about 0
The rotation by 180° about the point 0; it sends f to −f. Applying it twice gives the identity, so it is an involution. It exchanges the two roots of every square.
sheet
Used here only in the sense of one of the two halves of the "before" plane that the fold stacks on top of each other. Each sheet is an entire half-plane, not a flat drawing surface.
principal root
The square root chosen by a fixed rule, so that "take the root" becomes a map. The usual rule: the root with positive real part, and +i for −1. Any other consistent rule reaches a different half of the plane, but always exactly half.
sign
The choice between f and −f. Squaring forgets the sign; taking a root has to be told it.
unit
A quantity whose square is −1, 0 or +1 but which is not itself an ordinary real number. There are three: i (square −1), ε (square 0, ε ≠ 0) and j (square +1, j ≠ ±1). Each generates its own plane of numbers a + b·unit.
absolute
The conic a plane's measure is built on. Euclidean plane: the circle x² + y² = 1. Minkowski plane: the pair of crossing lines x² − y² = 0. Parabolic plane: the degenerate pair of parallel lines x² = 0, counted twice. The forms under diagram 2 are these three, with the constant dropped.
Euclidean, parabolic, Minkowski
The three planes of diagram 2, named after the geometry their unit produces. Parabolic is also called Galilean; Minkowski is also called hyperbolic or split-complex.
congruence (as used in "one congruence, two readings")
The square: the one value the fold produces. The two readings are the two roots that produce it.
