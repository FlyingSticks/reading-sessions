Two mirrors make a turn or a slide. The glide plate added a third mirror to a parallel pair and found a glide. This plate asks the question the glide plate left open: when do three mirrors make something simpler than a glide, namely one single mirror? The answer is exact, and it is the step that lets statements about points and lines be rewritten as statements about products of mirrors.

Throughout, “A then B then C” means: apply the mirror with line A first, the mirror with line B second, the mirror with line C third. Figure 1 shows the two cases in which three mirrors make one, and the reason. Figure 2 shows that in every other case they do not. Figure 3 uses the result to state “the point P is on the line m” without mentioning position at all.

1. Three mirrors that make one

Statement. If the three mirror lines A, B, C pass through one point, then A then B then C is a single mirror, and its line D passes through the same point. If A, B, C are parallel, then A then B then C is a single mirror, and its line D is parallel to them.

Reason. It uses two facts from earlier plates and nothing else.

  1. From the pair plate: the product of a pair of mirrors depends only on the crossing point and the angle from the first line to the second. So the pair A, B may be turned together about the crossing point, with the angle between them held fixed, and “A then B” does not change. (For a parallel pair, from the parallel-pair plate: the pair may be slid together, spacing held fixed.)
  2. From the One Generator page, step 1: a mirror applied twice gives every point back. It does nothing.

Now turn the pair A, B until B lies on C. This is possible because C passes through the same point. Call the new position of A by the name D. The product is now “D then C then C”. By fact 2, “C then C” does nothing. What is left is D alone.

In Figure 1, set the three lines with the first three sliders. Then move the fourth slider: it turns (or slides) the pair A, B together. Watch two things. The purple L‴ does not move at all. And at the end of the slider, B lies on C and A lies on the dashed gold line D.

The position of D can be read off the reason. In the first case, A is turned by the angle from B to C, so the direction of D is the direction of A, minus that of B, plus that of C. In the parallel case the same holds for positions across the lines.

2. Every other case

In Figure 2 the lines A and B cross at the point O and stay there. The line C can be moved off O. The product “A then B then C” is odd (three mirrors), so by the glide plate it is a mirror or a glide. The figure draws its axis and measures its slide. The slide is zero when C passes through O, and at no other setting.

The reason of section 1 shows where it fails. Turning the pair A, B about O can bring B parallel to C, but never onto C, because C does not pass through O. A parallel pair B, C is a slide, not nothing, and that slide is what remains.

One case is not in Figure 2: A and B parallel, at separation d, and C crossing them. Then “A then B” is a slide by 2d, straight across A and B (the parallel-pair plate). The mirror with line C follows. The part of that slide that runs along C is not undone by the mirror, and it is the slide of the glide. Its length is 2d × cos γ, where γ is the angle between C and the direction straight across A and B. It is zero only when γ is 90°, that is, when C is parallel to A and B.

So the statement of section 1 can be turned round. Three mirrors make a single mirror exactly when their lines pass through one point or are all parallel. In every other case they make a glide whose slide is not zero.

3. A point on a line, said with products

A half-turn about a point P is the product of two perpendicular mirrors whose lines cross at P (the pair plate, with the angle 90° doubled to 180°). So a point can be handled as a rule, just as a line can: the line m as the mirror with line m, the point P as the half-turn about P.

Consider the product “half-turn about P, then mirror with line m”. Write the half-turn as two perpendicular mirrors n, r through P, turned (by fact 1) so that r is parallel to m. The product is “n then r then m”.

  • If P is on m, then r lies on m, “r then m” does nothing, and the product is the single mirror with line n: the line through P perpendicular to m. Applied twice, it does nothing.
  • If P is not on m, then r and m are a parallel pair, and the product is a glide along n. Applied twice, it is a slide, not nothing.

In Figure 3 the slider moves P across m. The purple L is the probe after the product once. The dashed black outline is the probe after the product twice. The dashed outline returns to the green L at one setting only.

4. The dictionary

“Is an involution” means: applied twice it does nothing, and applied once it does something. (The rule that does nothing at all is left out. Plate 9, the involution, uses the word in the same way.) Each row below states a fact of position on the left and the same fact about a product on the right.

Said with positionSaid with products
A point P.A product of two mirrors that is an involution (a half-turn).
The lines A and B are perpendicular.“A then B” is an involution.
The point P is on the line m.“Half-turn about P, then mirror with line m” is an involution.
The lines A, B, C pass through one point, or are all parallel.“A then B then C” is an involution.

The right-hand column never mentions where anything is. It mentions only mirrors, their products, and whether a product undoes itself. Friedrich Bachmann built the whole of plane geometry from the right-hand column alone, taking the last row as an axiom instead of a theorem (Aufbau der Geometrie aus dem Spiegelungsbegriff, 1959). In that construction the mirror is the only object, and points, lines and incidence are all derived from it.

Why this is the step that limits the count to three

The earlier plates state that every congruence of the plane is a product of at most three mirrors. The limit comes from section 1. Take four mirrors A, B, C and a fourth, E, with A, B crossing at one point and C, E crossing at another. Turn the pair A, B until B passes through the crossing point of C and E. Then B, C, E pass through one point and make a single mirror. Four mirrors have become two. The cases with parallel pairs go the same way with a slide or a different choice of pair; they are not drawn here.

5. What is claimed

Rows 1 to 4 are statements about the ordinary Euclidean plane, with lengths measured by real numbers.

StatementPrice
Three mirrors whose lines pass through one point, or are all parallel, make a single mirror.Theorem (standard: the three-reflections theorem). The reason is given in section 1. Checked numerically.
In every other case three mirrors make a glide whose slide is not zero.Theorem (standard). Section 2 gives the reason for A, B crossing; checked numerically for that case. The case of A, B parallel and C crossing them is argued in the last paragraph of section 2 and checked by calculation (the slide is 2d × cos γ); it is not drawn.

The dictionary in the parabolic plane.Rows 1 and 2 fail. With the parabolic mirror of the three-pairs plate, the product of two mirrors moves each point along the ε-axis, and repeating the product doubles the move; so the product never undoes itself unless it does nothing. Hence no product of two parabolic mirrors is an involution: no two mirror lines are perpendicular, and no point is a product of two mirrors. Checked by calculation. Rows 3 and 4: not examined.
The dictionary in the Minkowski plane.Not claimed. Not examined on this plate.

6. Glossary

Mathematical words

Mirror with line m
As on the earlier plates.
A then B then C
The product of three mirrors, applied in the order written, left to right.
Half-turn about P
The turn by 180° about P. Each point X goes to the point X′ for which P is the midpoint of X and X′.
Glide, axis, slide
As on the glide plate. A glide is a slide along a line together with the mirror with that same line. The line is the axis. A glide whose slide is zero is a mirror.
Odd, even
A product of an odd number of mirrors is odd; of an even number, even. As on the earlier plates.
Involution
A rule that, applied twice, does nothing, and applied once does something. The same use as on plate 9, the involution.
Does nothing
Sends every point to itself. The standard name for the rule that does nothing is the identity.
Lines and their letters
A, B, C: mirror lines taken in that order. D: the line that is found. E: a fourth mirror line, in section 4 only. m, n, r: single lines in section 3. O: the point where A and B cross, as on the pair plate.

Drawing words

Probe
The solid green letter L. L′, L″, L‴ are the same letter after one, two and three mirrors. Green: the handedness of L. Purple: the opposite handedness.
Pale line
In Figure 1, a former position of A or B.
Dashed, dotted
Dashed gold: a line that is found, not given (D in Figure 1, the axis in Figure 2). Dotted: the line n in Figure 3.
