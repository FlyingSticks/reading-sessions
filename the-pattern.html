The Pattern

Four items, one template — and the book's title as its cleanest instance

This page develops one sentence from the porch: a shared law, a free gauge, a quarrel that mistook the gauge for the essence, and an impossibility theorem that forced the choice all along. It has two live instruments. The first holds the four items still while the cases change under them. The second draws the book's own case — one congruence, two readings — and puts perspective, cross-slit and parallel projection where your finger can find them. The theorems live in Chapter 2; this page keeps to human terms.

The four items

A law is the part of the situation that is not up to anybody: discovered, not chosen, and agreed on by all parties so completely that they never notice the agreement. Its crucial property is that it is mute. The sky's periods do not say where a month begins; the sphere does not say how to flatten itself; a family of lines does not say where along a line to write "depth 3." A law constrains everything and marks nothing, so a law alone is not yet an instrument.

A gauge is the graduation laid along the law to make it usable — a ruler, a calibration, a reading. "Free" means the law does not pick it: several gauges are equally lawful, so the choice adds information the law does not contain. And a gauge, once chosen, disappears. Everything downstream is calibrated in it and no result ever names it, so within a generation it is inherited as if it were part of the law.

The quarrel is what that invisibility costs. Two schools with different gauges each experience the other as wrong about reality, not different in convention — nobody can see their own datum — so the argument is conducted in essence-vocabulary: honest against lying, true against false, doctrine against drift. The freight is real, because the gauges genuinely pay different prices. The quarrel is wrongly located, one level too high, not wrongly felt. And persistence is the tell: facts eventually settle; conventions defended as facts never do.

The theorem is what dignifies the whole pattern. If some gauge could have everything, everyone would take the perfect one and "free" would only mean "not yet decided." The theorem proves no such gauge exists — so choosing is unavoidable, even for parties who thought they were describing the world. That is what "all along" means: Caesar obeyed the quarter-day before anyone measured it. And the theorem conserves the price: the defect can be smeared thin, piled up, or pushed to the edge, but never removed. Every gauge is a distribution of a fixed cost.

The title as an instance

Now read the book's title against the template. One congruence names the law: a single two-parameter family of lines in space, fixed by the two slits, mute about marks. Two readings names what must be added: each reading is a role for the lines plus a graduation along them. The glide draftsman reads the lines as edges — footprints on the floor, straight depth lines, a ruler that is his to choose: equal steps (Reach) or harmonic crowding toward a marked vanishing point (Turn). The camera reads the same lines as rays — sight lines through both slits, collapsed to marks on the window, with the ruler welded to foreshortening by the slit depths.

The camera's family is itself a family. Pull the two slits apart and it is the cross-slit camera; merge them and every ray passes one point — perspective; send both to infinity and the rays run parallel — parallel projection. The instrument below draws one box under both readings at once. The camera's two rates ru, rv are the slits' contraction speeds (rate 0 = slit at infinity); the front face sits on the picture plane at level 0 and is true-size under both readings — the one surface the two rulers share.

Camera as in Chapter 2: a point at depth λ images at (x/(1+ruλ), y/(1+rvλ)); ticks sit at depths 1–4 and the hollow crimson ring is the image of infinity — welded, the camera cannot decline to mark it. The glide draws the same depth edges straight from their footprints; Reach ticks equal steps and leaves infinity unmarked, Turn crowds harmonically toward its own crimson ring. Theorem 0 is why no ruler does both.

What the instrument shows

Watch the depth edges as the rates move. With two distinct slits they curve — that is the cross-slit signature, the curving depth lines of the camera views. Merge the rates and every image line straightens at once: perspective, and the four depth edges run to one vanishing point. Send both rates to zero and depth collapses entirely — the parallel camera looks straight down the depth axis and delivers the front elevation, true but flat. That collapse is why glide projection exists at all: the draftsman wants parallel light and visible depth, so he re-introduces depth by hand, gliding it laterally as straight lines from footprints — which is the whole 1984 construction in one clause.

And the seam is visible here in miniature. Off the special positions, the two pictures disagree about the shape of every depth edge: curve against line, the split. At perspective and at parallel, the camera's edges straighten and the two readings begin to agree about form, quarreling only about the ruler — and the ruler quarrel is exactly Theorem 0's forced choice, Reach letting infinity go, Turn and the welded camera marking it and paying in crowding. One law under both pictures; two gauges; the quarrel located where it belongs.
