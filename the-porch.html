The Porch

Three quarrels that were one quarrel

No one meets a picture plane for the first time. By the time you open a book like this one you have spent a lifetime looking through windows, standing on floors, keeping time and reading maps, and each of those practices has quietly taught you what a surface is for. The chapters past this page are written from the theorems up and take nothing on inheritance. But inheritance is what a reader brings, so it is worth three short stories to sort it — each a famous quarrel in another field, and each, it will turn out, the same quarrel.

The window and the wall

In 1435 Alberti told painters to treat the picture as an open window, and five centuries of Western picture-making stood on the sentence. The counter-tradition insisted on the opposite: a painting is a made thing, pigment on cloth, and its depth an illusion a serious eye should refuse. By the middle of the twentieth century the refusal had hardened into doctrine — flatness claimed as the one property painting owns outright — and Jasper Johns closed the argument's circle by painting flags and targets, subjects that are already flat, so that the picture and its object coincide and the window has nothing left to open onto. Meanwhile, on public television, Bob Ross kept the Albertian window cheerfully open for decades, pushing happy little trees into depth behind veils of titanium white.

Notice what both camps assume: that the two roles exhaust the plane. Either you look through the surface or you look at it. A third role never reached the table — the surface as a datum, a floor that a construction stands on. Builders have had that reading all along; every template, every level line, every stonecutter's trace assumes it. Painting never considered it because painting inherited its geometry from optics, and optics only offers the pane.

The year and the day

The law here is overhead, and nobody disputes it: the earth’s spin gives the day, its orbit gives the year, and the two periods are simply what they are. The graduation is the calendar — whole-day marks laid along the year. The obstruction is that the year is not a whole number of days. It runs about 365 days and a quarter, and not exactly a quarter, so no calendar closes: every one must choose where to hide the leftover, and the leftover can be moved but never removed. Caesar’s calendar smeared it as one added day every fourth year — a guess that overshoots the true year by eleven minutes, which no one notices in a lifetime and no one can miss after a millennium. By 1582 the spring equinox had drifted ten days up the calendar, and Gregory’s reform re-graduated the leftover more finely, ninety-seven leap days in four hundred years, striking the ten accumulated days out at a stroke. The quarrel that followed lasted longer than the error had. Protestant Europe kept the old count for generations rather than accept arithmetic from Rome; England held out until 1752, when eleven days vanished from September; Russia arrived in 1918; and the Orthodox churches keep Easter by the old reckoning still. A choice of graduation, prosecuted in the vocabulary of doctrine, by parties in perfect agreement about the sun.

The sphere and the sheet

The earth's surface is a law: the sphere carries its own geometry, and Gauss proved in 1827 that its curvature is intrinsic — it belongs to the surface, not to how the surface sits in space. The consequence is an impossibility: no flat map is faithful, and in particular none preserves both angles and areas. Every projection is a reading of the same globe, and every reading pays the theorem's price somewhere. Mercator chose angles, and gave sailors straight rhumb lines at the cost of a swollen north. Arno Peters chose areas, and in the 1970s the choice was prosecuted in moral vocabulary — one projection honest, the other a lie — though both are exact statements about the sphere, and each one's failure is the theorem's fee rather than a fault.

One quarrel, three costumes

The pattern is the same each time. There is a law nobody disputes: the day and the year, the sphere, the plane and what falls on it. There is a graduation that must be laid along the law before it yields calendars, maps or pictures. There is an impossibility theorem that forces the graduation to be a genuine choice — the leftover quarter-day, the intrinsic curvature — because no single scale can have everything. And there is a long public argument that mistook the chosen graduation for the essence of the art. The quarrels were real; materiality against illusion, the seasons against the received count, navigation against fairness all carry genuine freight. But each was conducted one level too high — over readings, by parties who agreed completely about the law beneath, and who could not see the free parameter they were fighting over.

The plane you are about to meet

This book is the geometric case, stated exactly and checked by instruments. Its law is a single two-parameter family of lines in space. Its two graduations have names, Reach and Turn, and its impossibility theorem opens Chapter 2: no scale on a straight line can mark equal steps and give infinity a finite mark. And its contested surface is the picture plane itself. The camera reading takes that plane as Alberti's window — a mark is where a sight line through two slits lands. The drawing reading takes the same plane as the builder's floor — a mark is a footprint, level zero of a measured slab, the place a straight depth line stands. The book will ask you to hold both readings at once, on one set of lines, and will show you exactly where they agree: a seam, thin and computable, where the window and the floor draw the same marks. Everything else in the chapters is the price and the profit of the split.
