Law, Gauge, Defect

the diagonal of a square, read against a counted base — Reach carried across a Turn

Terms

law
The fixed relation under interrogation. Here: the side of one square against a length laid along its diagonal. The law is drawn once and never refined — refinement happens to the instrument, not to it.
gauge
The refinable instrument: n equal steps on the side. Pure Reach — additive, equal-step, a ruler’s markings. Choosing n is choosing a gauge; raising n is refinement. The gauge need not double; it can grow by one.
Turn
The compass move. Each gauge mark is swept into an arc — a level set of distance from the corner — so that an oblique line can be scored at all. The compass measures nothing of its own: it is transport, Reach carried across a turn.
count
Arcs pierced: m = ⌊nL⌋. The whole-step part of the law in this gauge. It brackets the law: m/n ≤ L < (m+1)/n.
defect
What remains past the last whole step — the blue overshoot. Not an error in the drawing but the law’s answer to the gauge, payable in two currencies (below).

The defect’s two currencies

Measure the same blue remainder two ways. In absolute length it is squeezed under refinement — it can never exceed one gauge step, 1/n, so it drifts toward zero. In the gauge’s own unit — as a fraction of the current step — it is the fractional part of nL, and it does not shrink at all. It wanders the interval and never settles. Refinement re-denominates the defect; it never diminishes it.

Absolute currency: the defect (purple) under its shrinking ceiling 1/n (dashed). The approximation story — the fractions creep toward the law.

Reading

The base is pure Reach: equal steps, counted. The compass is the Turn that carries those steps across to the diagonal — the arcs land exactly where they should, and the piercing count does not care about direction, only depth. Whatever fails here, it is not the transport.

What the drawing interrogates is the law: does the diagonal’s length belong to the base’s gauge? Each gauge answers not yet. The count lands strictly between two arcs, and the blue remainder is the witness.

A rational law is one whose defect eventually posts a zero: switch to 7⁄5 and watch the ledger clear at every fifth gauge — at those gauges the law’s mark falls exactly on an arc, and side and diagonal-length share a common measure. √2 never clears. Its defect is positive in every gauge in the family, forever: a law that keeps a permanent balance owing in every gauge you offer it. That standing debt is what incommensurability looks like on a drafting board.
