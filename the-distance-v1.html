Plates 8 to 12 kept every mirror line through the point 0. That left two things out. A pair of mirror lines may cross at some other point. And a pair may not cross at all: the parallel pair of plate 4, whose measure is a distance, d. The measure plate read the measure of a crossing pair as a cross-ratio on a horizon, and said nothing of distance.

This plate supplies both. Section 1 moves the crossing point away from 0 and finds that nothing changes. Section 2 sends the crossing point away without limit and watches the crossing pair become a parallel pair. Section 3 looks at the same thing on one line, and finds the parabolic case of the measure plate there, in all three planes. Section 4 follows the product of the two mirrors. Section 5 says where the unit of length comes in.

1. A pair that crosses away from 0

Let A and B be two mirror lines that cross at a point O, which need not be 0. As on the measure plate, neither has the direction of the 1-axis, which has no reading, and neither is self-paired; in the Minkowski plane both are flatter than the diagonals. Draw through 0 the line parallel to A and the line parallel to B. Parallel lines have one direction, and a reading belongs to a direction (absolute plate, section 1). So the readings tA and tB are those of the two parallels through 0, the cross-ratio R of the measure plate is formed from them as before, and the measure from A to B follows by that plate's rule. The measure of a crossing pair belongs to the two directions. The crossing point has no part in it.

There is a second way to the same number, which does not go through 0. The lines through O can be read on the horizon directly: each crosses it somewhere. Take the four lines through O that matter, A, B and the two self-paired lines, and take the four points where they cross the horizon. In the Minkowski plane all four are on the page. Their cross-ratio is R again. The reason is the involution plate's: four lines through one point cut every line that crosses all four, and does not pass through that point, in four points with one and the same cross-ratio.

2. Sending the crossing point away

Now fix two things: a line ℓ, and the two points of ℓ where A and B cross it. Call those points HA and HB. Then move O away from ℓ. The lines A and B must turn to keep passing through HA and HB, and the further O goes, the more nearly they agree in direction. With O gone altogether they are a parallel pair.

For the figure, ℓ is the axis through 0 that carries g (the i-axis, ε-axis or j-axis), drawn upright. The point O is on the 1-axis, to the left of 0, at the distance ρ. The points HA and HB are at the heights hA = 0.6 and hB = 1.8 on ℓ. This choice is made so that ℓ lies in the across direction of the parallel pair that is being approached, in all three planes: the across direction of the 1-axis is the axis that carries g.

The line A rises by hA over the run ρ, so its slope is sA = hA ÷ ρ, and its reading is one over that (the slope and the reading of one line multiply to 1). The same for B. In the Minkowski plane ρ must be greater than both heights, so that both lines are flatter than the diagonals; the slider starts at ρ = 3. Write φ for the measure from A to B, found by the rule of the measure plate. Write d for the separation of the parallel pair that is being approached: d = hB − hA, the length of the segment from HA to HB, here 1.2.

As ρ grows, φ shrinks toward nothing. But the product ρ × φ does not: ρ × φ comes closer and closer to d. In the parabolic plane it is equal to d at every ρ.

ρ: distance of O from ℓ

Why

Euclidean. The measure is the angle φ at O, in radians. Draw the circle about O through 0; its radius is ρ. The piece of it between A and B has the length ρ × φ: that is what a radian is. The segment of ℓ from HA to HB has the length d. As ρ grows the circle flattens against ℓ, the arc lies down on the segment, and the two lengths come together. In numbers, φ = arctan(hB ÷ ρ) − arctan(hA ÷ ρ), and the arctangent of a small number is nearly that number.

Parabolic. The measure is the difference of slopes (three-pairs plate): φ = hB ÷ ρ − hA ÷ ρ = d ÷ ρ. So ρ × φ = d, with nothing left over, at every ρ. The curve that stands in for the circle about O through 0 is, in this plane, an upright line through 0: it is ℓ. The arc and the segment are the same piece of the page.

Minkowski. The measure is φ = artanh(hB ÷ ρ) − artanh(hA ÷ ρ), by the rule of the measure plate and the standard fact used there. The inverse hyperbolic tangent of a small number is nearly that number, so ρ × φ comes to d as before. The curve that stands in for the circle is one piece of a hyperbola about O through 0; it bends away from ℓ to the other side, and flattens against it as ρ grows.

How close: in the Euclidean and Minkowski planes, ρ × φ differs from d by less than (|hA|³ + |hB|³) ÷ ρ² once ρ is at least three times the larger height. The reason rests on two standard facts, used here and not proved. For any slope s, arctan s differs from s by at most |s|³ ÷ 3. For a slope of size at most one third, artanh s differs from s by at most (3 ÷ 8) × |s|³. Put s = h ÷ ρ and multiply by ρ: the measure of one line, times ρ, differs from its height h by at most |h|³ ÷ (3 × ρ²), or (3 ÷ 8) × |h|³ ÷ ρ². Do this for A and for B and add the two errors. Both are inside the bound stated, with room to spare.

3. The same thing on one line

The line ℓ can be used as the horizon was used. It is a line on which the lines through a point are read: the point is O, the line stands at the distance ρ in place of 1, and it is upright in place of level. The four lines through O that matter cross ℓ at four heights:

In the Minkowski plane γ is 1, and the two self-paired lines cross ℓ at the heights +ρ and −ρ: the points N+ and N− of Figure 2. In the Euclidean plane γ is i, the two heights are not ordinary numbers, and nothing is drawn; the arithmetic is the same. In the parabolic plane the one self-paired line through O is parallel to ℓ and does not cross it at all.

By the reason given in section 1, the cross-ratio of the four heights is R. (The formula needs γ not to be 0, so it is for the Euclidean and Minkowski planes; the parabolic plane was settled in section 2 by one division.)

R = ((hA − ρ ÷ γ) × (hB + ρ ÷ γ)) ÷ ((hA + ρ ÷ γ) × (hB − ρ ÷ γ)).

Now let ρ grow, with hA and hB fixed. The points N+ and N− leave the page, one upward and one downward. A line has one point at infinity, not two (involution plate: a point at infinity is a direction, and ℓ has one direction). So the two self-paired points of ℓ are heading for the same point. When they are there, ℓ carries a pairing with a single self-paired point, counted twice. That is the parabolic pairing of the absolute plate, and it has arrived on ℓ whatever the plane.

The measure plate has already dealt with that case. With the two self-paired points together, R is 1 and holds nothing; what survives is the last trace of R as they close, and it is a difference. Here the algebra is the same single line. Subtract 1 from the formula for R, multiply out the top, and multiply by ρ ÷ (2γ):

ρ × (R − 1) ÷ (2γ) = (hB − hA) × ρ² ÷ ((ρ + γ × hA) × (ρ − γ × hB)).

As ρ grows, the fraction on the right comes to 1, and what is left is hB − hA = d.

The two limits are one limit. Suppose neither hA nor hB is 0, which is to say that neither line passes through 0. Read each point of ℓ by one over its height. Then HA and HB have the readings 1 ÷ hA and 1 ÷ hB, the two self-paired points have the readings +γ ÷ ρ and −γ ÷ ρ, and the formula for R above becomes the formula of the measure plate, letter for letter, with γ ÷ ρ in place of γ. On the measure plate the two self-paired readings closed on 0 because γ shrank. Here they close on 0 because ρ grows. The measure that survives there was 1 ÷ tB − 1 ÷ tA; here that is hB − hA.

So a distance is a measure of the parabolic kind, in every one of the three planes. The planes differ in how the lines through a point are paired: three kinds. They do not differ in how the points along a mirror's across line are measured: one kind.

4. The product

The product “A then B” of a crossing pair keeps O in place and turns the plane about it through twice the measure, 2 × φ, counted counter-clockwise when φ is positive, as on the measure plate (plates 3 and 8; a turn in the Euclidean plane, and the congruence that takes its place in the other two). The product of a parallel pair is a slide through twice the separation, 2 × d, in the across direction (plate 4).

Figure 2 shows the one becoming the other. The probe X is carried by the two mirrors to X″. The grey cross marks where the slide of the parallel pair would put it. At small ρ the two are well apart. As ρ grows, X″ settles onto the cross. A slide is a turn about a point that has gone to infinity.

The reason, in the Euclidean plane. Let the probe stand at the distance x to the right of ℓ and at the height y. From O it is ρ + x across and y up. A turn about O through the angle 2 × φ moves it up by (ρ + x) × sin(2φ) − y × (1 − cos(2φ)) and sideways by −(ρ + x) × (1 − cos(2φ)) − y × sin(2φ). As ρ grows, 2 × φ shrinks, ρ × 2φ comes to 2 × d (section 2), the sine of a small angle is nearly the angle, and 1 − cos of a small angle is nearly half its square. So the first expression comes to 2 × d and the second to 0. This holds for every probe point, not only the one drawn. In the Minkowski plane the same holds with the hyperbolic sine in place of the sine, the hyperbolic cosine less 1 in place of 1 − cos, and a plus sign before every term.

In the parabolic plane X″ is straight above X at every ρ, at the height 2 × φ × (the distance of X from the upright line through O). That distance is ρ plus the fixed distance of X from ℓ, so the height comes to 2 × d as ρ grows.

5. Where the unit comes in

The measure φ of a crossing pair is a plain number. It needs no unit of length: it is read from a cross-ratio. A distance d needs a unit. In section 2 the unit arrives with ρ: d is what ρ × φ comes to, and ρ is a length, measured along the 1-axis.

So the limit does not make a length out of nothing. It takes a length along one line and yields a length along another line, in the across direction. What connects the two is φ, and φ depends on the decree. The three decrees of this thread do not show that: each has g² equal to −1, 0 or +1, and in all three ρ × φ comes to the same length on the page, d, as the ledger of Figure 2 shows. A decree of another size does show it. Take g² = −c², with c a positive number other than 1, so that the form is x² + c² × y². The measure of a line of slope s, from the 1-axis, is then arctan(c × s), and ρ × φ comes to c × d. The lines on the page are the same and ρ is the same; the length that comes out is c times as great. Lengths along one line, or along parallel lines, can be compared with no decree at all, by ratios. Lengths along two lines that are not parallel cannot. The decree is what compares them.

The document “A Projective Plane plus Signs” holds that a construction needs one ruler reading and no more, and that it should be named. On this plate it is ρ.

One thing more is recorded and not pursued. Throughout this plate d is the plain length of the segment from HA to HB on the page. Only in the Euclidean plane is the form of that segment d². In the Minkowski plane it is −d²: ℓ is steeper than the diagonals. In the parabolic plane it is 0: the form of every upright step is 0, and ℓ is upright. So in the parabolic plane the form cannot give the length d at all; the measure gives it, as ρ × φ, and nothing else on the plate does. This plate says nothing more about the sign of the form or about its vanishing.

7. Glossary

Mathematical words

Reading, horizon, self-paired, across direction
As on the absolute plate.
γ, R, measure from A to B
As on the measure plate.
O
The point where A and B cross. As on plates 3, 4 and 6. It is a point of the page and is not the number 0.
Parallel
Two lines are parallel when they have one direction, which is to say when they do not cross.
Slope, s
Of a line: its rise divided by its run, y ÷ x for a step along it. The reading of the same direction is x ÷ y, so slope × reading = 1.
φ
The measure from A to B, by the rule of the measure plate. A plain number. Euclidean: the angle, in radians on this plate (the ledger also shows degrees). Parabolic: the difference of slopes. Minkowski: half the logarithm of R.
ρ
The distance of O from ℓ, measured along the 1-axis. A length. This plate's own letter.
ℓ
The line on which A and B are held: the axis through 0 that carries g. This plate's own letter.
hA, hB
The heights, above 0, at which A and B cross ℓ. Numbers.
d
The separation of a parallel pair, as on plate 4. Here d = hB − hA.
Radian
The unit of angle for which an arc of a circle has the length radius × angle. A full turn is about 6.283 radians; 1 radian is about 57.3°.
arctan, artanh
The rules that give the Euclidean and the Minkowski measure of a line from its slope (measure plate, in other words: the angle of the unit point, and the logarithm of its λ). For a small slope each gives back nearly the slope itself.
Turn, slide
The product of a crossing pair; the product of a parallel pair. Each is a congruence made of two mirrors.
Comes to
“u comes to v as ρ grows” means: the difference between u and v can be made as small as wanted by taking ρ large enough.
