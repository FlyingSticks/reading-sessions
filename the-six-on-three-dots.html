The Six on Three Dots

The plate Correspondences and Circuits (correspondences-and-circuits.html) found six reversible correspondences on three dots. This plate does one of them and then another. The six turn out to be closed under "then", and the table of results is the same table that the six maps of the book's Chapter 4 obey. Index: the primer (the-primer.html).

What the table says

  • Closed. Every cell holds one of the six. Doing one and then another never produces anything new.
  • Once each. Each row holds each of the six exactly once, and so does each column.
  • The identity changes nothing, whether it is done first or second.
  • Each exchange, done twice, is the identity. That, and moving at least one dot, is what "involution" means. The identity, done twice, is also the identity, but it moves nothing and is not counted. Each circuit, done twice, is the other circuit; done three times, it is the identity.
  • Each of the six can be undone by one of the six: the identity and each exchange by itself, each circuit by the other circuit.
  • Two different exchanges, done in turn, always make a circuit. Which circuit depends on the order. The circuits need not be supplied separately; the exchanges produce them.
  • Order. Of the 36 ordered choices, 18 give the same result in either order and 18 do not.
  • The tinted pattern is a rule of signs. Call an exchange "minus" and the other three "plus". Then minus then minus is plus, minus then plus is minus, and plus then plus is plus, in every cell. This is the sign of the plate Level, and Square to the Card, met again where there is no space and no mirror, only three dots.

A family of reversible correspondences that is closed under "then", contains the identity, and contains for each member the one that undoes it, is called a group. The six on three dots are a group. Its standard name is the symmetric group on three things, written S₃.

An observation about the drawing, not about the dots. If the three dots are set at the corners of a triangle with three equal sides, as here, each exchange is the mirror of that triangle in the line through the dot it sends to itself, and each circuit is a turn of the triangle through one third of a full turn. "Two different exchanges make a circuit" is then "two mirrors make a turn".

Toward the book

  • The three dots have names in the book. Its entry Degenerate orbit gives the set 0, 1, ∞ and says that on the board these are the picture plane, the station and the plane at infinity. Its entry Directing gauge says the 3-cycle ω carries picture plane → station → plane at infinity → back. Since ω is 1/(1−λ), which sends 0 → 1 → ∞ → 0, the two entries agree if 0 is the picture plane, 1 the station and ∞ the plane at infinity. That assignment is this plate's reading of the two entries together.
  • "Two involutions force the 3-cycles to exist." The book's 3-cycle entry says so. On the dots it is the sixth line of "What the table says".
  • The order of writing differs. The book writes ρτ = ω, with ρ the Reciprocal and τ the Complement. Computed: the Complement and then the Reciprocal gives 1/(1−λ), which is ω; the other order gives the other 3-cycle. So in the book's writing the map on the right is done first. On this plate "A then B" is written in the order in which the two are done.
  • The book's τ∘σ = ω. With τ the Complement, this holds when σ is the Third: the Third and then the Complement gives ω. The glossary does not say which map σ is, so that identification is computed, not quoted.
  • A second set of three dots. The six maps also rearrange the three values −1, ½ and 2 in all six ways (the book's Harmonic orbit). Either set of three values can serve as the dots.

Terms

The plain words are the plate's own. The standard name, for looking things up elsewhere, follows each in grey.

top, left, right
The names of the three dots, taken from where they are drawn. Standard: labels of a three-element set.
reversible correspondence
A rule sending each dot to one dot, with each dot reached exactly once; see the plate Correspondences and Circuits. Standard: a bijection; on finitely many things, a permutation.
exchange
A reversible correspondence with exactly one pair, every other dot being sent to itself. Standard: a transposition.
circuit of three
Three dots, each sent to the next and the last back to the first. Standard: a 3-cycle.
A then B
The correspondence got by doing A first and B second. Standard: the composite of A and B. Most books write it B∘A, with the one done first on the right.
table of "then"
The table of figure 3: row done first, column done second, cell the result. Standard: a composition table, or Cayley table.
closed
Said of a family when doing any member and then any member gives a member of the family. Standard: closed under composition.
undoes
B undoes A when "A then B" is the identity. Standard: B is the inverse of A.
the order matters
Said of A and B when "A then B" and "B then A" are different. Standard: A and B do not commute. A group in which this happens is called non-commutative, or non-abelian.
group
A family of reversible correspondences that is closed, contains the identity, and contains for each member the one that undoes it. Standard: a group of permutations. The six on three dots are the symmetric group S₃.
plus, minus
On this plate only: "minus" for an exchange, "plus" for the identity and the circuits. Standard: the sign, or parity, of a permutation: odd for minus, even for plus.
λ
The Greek letter lambda, the book's letter for a cross-ratio: one number, which may also be ∞. Standard: a point of the projective line.
the six maps
λ, 1−λ, 1/λ, 1/(1−λ), λ/(λ−1) and (λ−1)/λ. Standard: the anharmonic group; each member is a Möbius map, also called a fractional linear map.
the match
The pairing of each map with the dot diagram showing what it does to 0, 1 and ∞, which respects "then". Standard: an isomorphism of groups.
