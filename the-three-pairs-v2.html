The pair plate showed one fact in the Euclidean plane: two mirrors whose lines cross at θ compose to a turn by 2θ. The fold page showed three planes, one for each decree g² = −1, 0, +1. This plate draws the pair in all three. The construction is the same each time — mirror line A, mirror line B, a probe point sent across A and then across B — and only the decree changes. Figure 1 shows what one mirror is in each plane. Figure 2 shows the pair. The table after it sets the three results side by side. A glossary follows; drawing words and mathematical words are kept apart.

1. One mirror, in each plane

In every plane the mirror with line m is the same kind of map: each point of m stays where it is, and every other point X has a partner X′ such that m passes through the midpoint of the segment XX′. What differs is the direction of that segment. Call it the across direction. In the Euclidean plane it is perpendicular to m. In the other two planes it is not.

One drawing rule gives the across direction in all three planes: it is the direction of the tangent to the unit locus at the point where m crosses it. A tangent to a circle is perpendicular to the radius, which is the Euclidean case. In the parabolic plane the unit locus is itself a straight line, so its tangent is that line. In the Minkowski plane the tangent to the hyperbola leans toward m.

Taken further on the involution plate, plate 9, and the absolute, plate 10: the across direction is the direction of the centre of a harmonic pairing, and the one job of a decree is to choose it.

Move the slider and compare three directions in each panel: the short black tangent, the dotted line, and the segment XX′. They stay parallel. In the Euclidean panel the dotted line stays perpendicular to m. In the parabolic panel the dotted line does not move at all: every mirror pairs points along the ε-axis, whatever its line. In the Minkowski panel the dotted line closes toward m like the other blade of a pair of scissors, the two blades always placed evenly about the diagonal.

What all three keep is the form of the plane: the form has the same value at X and at X′. Only the Euclidean mirror also keeps the distances you would measure on the drawing with a ruler. The other two are mirrors of their own planes, not of the page.

2. The pair, in each plane

Now two mirrors. Mirror line A is the 1-axis. Mirror line B is set by the slider. The probe P starts on the unit locus, is sent across A to P′, then across B to P″. The dashed line A′ is where the mirror with line B sends the line A, as in Figure 2 of the pair plate.

A way to read it: start on the Euclidean button, where everything is familiar. Then press the other two buttons without touching the slider, and note what stays and what changes.

Three things hold in every plane, and the ledger shows each of them as a measured number, not as a rule.

The doubling. The two shaded sectors have equal area: B divides the region from A to A′ into two equal parts. So the measure from P to P″ is twice the measure from A to B. In the Euclidean plane this is the 2θ of the pair plate, and "equal area" is the same statement as "equal angle". In the other two planes there is no angle on the drawing that doubles, but the area still does.

The reason is the same in all three planes, and it is the bisector argument of the pair plate restated for area. The mirror with line B keeps 0 and keeps b, sends the unit point of A to the unit point of A′, and sends the unit locus to itself. So it carries the darker sector exactly onto the lighter one. A mirror has determinant −1: it reverses orientation but keeps the size of every area. Two regions that a mirror carries onto each other therefore have equal area.

The track. P, P′ and P″ all lie on the unit locus, because each mirror keeps the form. As the slider moves, P″ runs along that locus and nowhere else: a circle, a straight line parallel to the ε-axis, or one branch of a hyperbola. This is the unit-locus row of diagram 2 on the fold page, now read as the path a probe is carried along by a pair of mirrors.

The sign. Each mirror has determinant −1; the product has determinant +1. One mirror is odd, a pair is even, in all three planes.

The connection to the fold is the small ringed dots. The one on B is the unit point b of that line. The one on A′ is the number b², the square of b in that plane's arithmetic. The product "A then B" is: multiply every point by b². The pair is the root; what it does is the square. And b and −b lie on the same line B, so the line cannot tell the two roots apart, which is the fold.

Taken further on the root plate, plate 11: the same statement for any two lines A and B, and where the two roots can and cannot be told apart.

The last row is the null locus of the fold page, reappearing a third time. A line can be a mirror line only if it is different from its own across direction. In the parabolic plane the ε-axis is the across direction of every mirror, so it can never be a mirror line. In the Minkowski plane a line on the diagonal is its own across direction — the two scissor blades have closed — so the diagonals cannot be mirror lines. In the Euclidean plane a line is never perpendicular to itself, so every line through 0 can be a mirror line.

What is and is not drawn

Drawn: mirror lines through 0 only; in the Minkowski plane only lines flatter than the diagonal, and only the right-hand branch of the hyperbola as track. Not drawn: Minkowski mirror lines steeper than the diagonal (they exist, and pair points along a direction flatter than the diagonal); pairs of mirror lines that do not pass through one point; the slider range is kept short so that P″ stays inside the frame. Checked numerically before issue, for all three planes over the slider range: the two sectors' areas agree; the measure from P to P″ is twice the measure from A to B; the form is 1 at P, P′, P″; the determinants are −1, −1, +1. Also checked numerically: the tangent to the unit locus at a line's unit point is parallel to that line's across direction; the mirror with line B carries the boundary of the darker sector onto the boundary of the lighter one. Not checked against a published source: the wording "across direction", which is this plate's own. Removed from version 1 as stated but not shown: the remark that only the Euclidean track closes up and its link to the seam of the fold page. Also removed: the names these maps carry in mechanics and relativity, which lead away from this plate's subject.

Glossary

Drawing words and mathematical words are kept apart. Terms carried over from the fold page and the pair plate keep their meaning.

decree
The choice of value for g²: −1, 0 or +1. Everything else in the arithmetic of a plane is ordinary; the decree is the one thing chosen.
plane (with generator g)
The numbers x + y·g. Drawn with x to the right along the 1-axis and y upward along the g-axis. The g-axis is called the ε-axis in the parabolic plane.
form
The quantity x² − g²·y². Euclidean: x² + y². Parabolic: x². Minkowski: x² − y².
unit locus
The set where the form equals 1. Circle; the two lines x = ±1; hyperbola.
mirror with line m (of a plane)
The map of that plane which keeps every point of m where it is, pairs every other point X with a partner X′ so that the midpoint of XX′ lies on m and the segment XX′ lies in the across direction, and keeps the form. Done twice it gives the identity. In the Euclidean plane this is the mirror of the earlier plates.
across direction (this plate's word)
The direction of every segment XX′ joining a point to its partner under one mirror. If the mirror line is the line of the number u, the across direction is the line of g·u. Drawing rule: the direction of the tangent to the unit locus at the mirror line's unit point. Euclidean: perpendicular to the mirror line. Parabolic: the ε-axis. Minkowski: the mirror line's image under the Euclidean mirror with the diagonal x = y as its line.
tangent (to the unit locus at a point)
The straight line that touches the unit locus at that point and has the locus's direction there. For a locus that is already a straight line, the tangent is the line itself.
partner
Of a point X under a mirror: the point X′ the mirror sends it to. The partner of X′ is X.
pair
Two mirrors of the same plane, taken in order. Here their lines both pass through 0.
product "A then B"
The map obtained by doing the mirror with line A first and the mirror with line B second. "Product" is the standard word; no numbers are being multiplied until the map is recognised as "multiply every point by b²".
probe
A point (or figure) watched in order to see what a map does. A probe lying on a mirror line does not detect that mirror; P is placed off both lines.
unit point of a line
A point where a line through 0 meets the unit locus. Each such line has two, b and −b. The small ringed dots mark the one on the right; in Figure 2 they are labelled 1 (on A), b (on B) and b² (on A′).
sector (drawing word)
The shaded region bounded by two segments from 0 to the unit locus and the piece of the unit locus between them. In the parabolic plane it is a triangle.
measure (from one line to another)
Twice the area of the sector between their unit points, counted positive counter-clockwise. This one definition serves all three planes. Euclidean: it equals the angle in radians. Parabolic: it equals the difference of slopes. Minkowski: it is the hyperbolic angle. The slider sets it; the ledger measures it back from the drawing.
track (drawing word)
The path P″ runs along as the slider moves. It is part of the unit locus.
determinant
The factor by which a map multiplies signed area. −1 for each mirror: area kept, orientation reversed. +1 for the product of a pair. Its sign is the sign of the map in the mirror-count ledger.
keeps the form / keeps drawn distance
Two different properties. Every mirror on this plate keeps the form of its own plane. Only the Euclidean mirrors keep the distance measured on the drawing, that is, only they are isometries of the page.
dotted, dashed, ringed (drawing words)
Dotted line: an across direction or a diagonal, shown for reference. Short black segment in Figure 1: a piece of a tangent. Dashed line: the image line A′. Ringed dot: a small circle with an empty centre; not the algebraic word "ring".
