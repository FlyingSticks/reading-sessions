mirror → product → sign → measure

Every congruence of the plane is a product of mirrors. The sign of a congruence is the count of its mirrors, even or odd. The measure comes from a pair of mirrors. That is the thesis of the first plate, and every later plate takes up one part of it. This page gives the order in which the plates are meant to be read, what each one adds, what it hands on, and the conventions they share. It claims nothing of its own.

1. The plates, in reading order

Plates 1 to 6 stay in the Euclidean plane. Plates 7 to 13 work in three planes at once. Each plate carries a line under its title giving its number, the plate before and the plate after, and a table headed “What is claimed” that prices each of its statements. The side plates have no such table.

PlateWhat it addsWhat it hands on
1One GeneratorThe mirror is the one generator. Sign: the count of mirrors, even or odd. Measure: the separation of a pair. The five congruences of the plane.Sign to plate 2. The crossing pair to plate 3, the parallel pair to plate 4, the glide to plate 5. Three excursions: side plates A, D and E.
2Compose, Multiply, Sign
(optional)		Plate 1, step 2, already states the result; the thread can go from plate 1 to plate 3. This plate argues it. Maps compose; their signs multiply. The sign belongs to the map and not to the recipe of mirrors; the determinant is the independent witness. The sign has two values and no path between them.The determinant row in every later ledger.
3The pairTwo mirrors whose lines cross at separation φ make a turn by 2φ. The doubling comes from one property: a mirror line bisects. The product remembers the separation and the crossing point, and not which pair made it. Reversing the order reverses the turn.No crossing point: plate 4. “The pair may be turned together”: used as a fact on plate 6. The same pair in three planes: plate 8.
4The parallel pairTwo parallel mirrors at separation d make a slide by 2d. The slide is what the turn approaches as the crossing point leaves the page.A third mirror added: plate 5. “The pair may be slid together”: used on plate 6.
5The glideThree mirrors, two parallel and one perpendicular to them, make a glide: odd, yet with a continuous measure. A glide done twice is a slide. A slide has one even root and a family of odd roots.Three mirrors in general, left open here: plate 6.
6The third mirrorThree mirrors make a single mirror exactly when their lines pass through one point or are all parallel; otherwise a glide. From this: “a point is on a line”, “two lines are perpendicular” and “three lines pass through one point” are each said with products alone. Four mirrors reduce to two.Left open: the Minkowski plane. In the parabolic plane two of the four dictionary rows are known to fail.
7The foldSquaring sends f and −f to one value: one square, two roots. One question, what squares to −1, 0 or +1, gives three planes, each with a unit locus and a null locus. The seam: no continuous choice of root all the way round 0.The three planes to plate 8. The two roots to plate 11. Its words “square” and “root” are already used on plate 5.
8Three pairsThe pair of plate 3 drawn in the Euclidean, parabolic and Minkowski planes. One mirror in each plane: the midpoint rule holds in all three; the across direction changes. Measure as twice a sector area; the doubling holds in all three. The unit points 1, b, b².What the across direction is: plate 9. The pair as a number, for any two lines: plate 11.
9The involutionOne level below the mirror: a pairing that undoes itself. The harmonic pairing with line m and centre K, and why the value −1 is forced. Send K to infinity and harmonic becomes midpoint. Choose the direction of K and the pairing is the mirror of one of the three planes.Its use of “involution” is the one plate 6 already relies on. How a decree chooses the direction: plate 10.
10The absoluteA decree is a pairing of directions, read on a horizon: t × t′ = g². The centre K of plate 9 lies in the partner direction of m. Three kinds of pairing: pairs that interlock, pairs that nest, pairs that meet at one point. The self-paired directions are the absolute: the null locus, the lines that cannot be mirrors, and the directions in which the unit locus leaves the page.Where the unit locus leaves the page: plate 11. The measure on the horizon: plate 12. Left open: mirror lines that do not pass through 0.
11The rootThe mirror in numbers: z → b² × z̄. The pair: z → q² × z, with q = b ÷ a. The bar is the sign; the point of the unit locus is the measure. The two roots q, −q are joined in the Euclidean plane and separated in the other two.Left open: mirror lines that do not pass through 0; Minkowski lines steeper than the diagonal.
12The measureThe measure of a pair read on the horizon, from the cross-ratio R of the two lines with the two self-paired readings. Minkowski: half the logarithm of R. Euclidean: R is the number q² of plate 11, and the measure is half its angle. Parabolic: R is always 1, and the measure is its last trace as the absolute closes to one point. R = −1 is the right angle in one plane and no measure at all in another.Left open: lines that do not pass through 0; the measure of a parallel pair, which is a distance.
13The distanceMirror lines that do not pass through 0. The measure of a crossing pair belongs to the two directions, wherever the lines cross. Send the crossing point O away to the distance ρ, holding the lines on a line ℓ: ρ × φ comes to the separation d of a parallel pair, and the turn comes to a slide. On ℓ the two self-paired points run together at its one point at infinity, so a distance is a measure of the parabolic kind in all three planes. The unit of length comes in with ρ.Left open: Minkowski lines steeper than the diagonals; a parallel pair whose lines are not level on the page; the sign of the form of a Minkowski segment.

5. Colours

There are three colour codes. Each plate's own caption governs; this table only says which code a plate follows.

CodePlatesColours
First1, 2, 3; side plates A, D, Eteal: mirror line A, or mirror 1. gold: mirror line B, or mirror 2. purple: a third mirror, or the image in the other order. black: the figure. grey: an intermediate image. red: the final image.
Second4, 5, 6black: line A. red-brown: line B, or the single line m. blue: line C. gold, dashed: a line that is found. green: a figure with the handedness of L. purple: a figure with the opposite handedness.
Third7 to 12purple: the Euclidean plane. grey: the parabolic plane. green: the Minkowski plane. red-brown: the single line m, or line B. black: line A, the probe, and the small dots on the unit locus.

Purple therefore means three things: a third mirror under the first code, the opposite handedness under the second, the Euclidean plane under the third. Green means the handedness of L under the second code and the Minkowski plane under the third. No plate mixes two codes. Side plates B and C have their own keys.

WordThe uses

rootOf a number (plates 7, 10). Of a map (plate 5): a map whose square, the map done twice, is the given map.
involutionA rule that, applied twice, does nothing and, applied once, does something. Plates 1 and 7 use the word of the mirror and of the half-turn, which agrees. The rule that does nothing is not counted (plates 6, 9).
absolute, null locusPlate 7 kept the two words apart. Plate 10 shows they name the same lines: the null locus is the lines through 0 on which the form is 0, and the absolute is the directions of those lines.
horizonPlate 10: the line one unit above 0 on which directions are read. The “Two Absolutes” page and the Measure and Sign documents: the same construction. The point 0 is their station point SP; the point of the horizon directly above 0 is their foot (Q or P); the height 1 is their distance A; a reading t is the signed length from the foot to a vanishing point V. Their rule for perpendicular directions is the rule of plate 10 with A in place of 1: the product of the two lengths is g² × A². Their A is a length, not the mirror line A of this thread. Plate 9 uses no horizon; its line h is a different line. Plate 13 uses the line ℓ as a second horizon, for the point O: upright, at the distance ρ.
congruenceA map that keeps every distance. Plate 7's glossary also records the special use in the phrase “one congruence, two readings”.
