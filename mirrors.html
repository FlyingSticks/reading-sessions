One Generator

Mirrors, sign, and measure in the plane — a reorganization of the pin sketches

Every congruence of the plane is a product of mirrors. The sign of a congruence is the count of its mirrors, mod 2. The measure of a congruence is the separation of a pair of mirrors. A single mirror has sign and no measure.

The pin sketches asked a question the textbook does not start from: what can a figure tell about the map that was done to it? This page keeps that question and puts the standard structure under it. Five steps, each with a diagram you can operate, then the complete list of plane congruences, then a glossary. All terms used here are defined in the glossary; a drawing word (pin, head, fold line) is never used for a mathematical object, and a mathematical word (mirror, half-turn, sign) is never used for a drawing.

1. One generator: the mirror Cartan–Dieudonné

A mirror is a map of the plane determined by one line m, its mirror line: every point of m stays where it is, and every other point P goes to the one point P′ for which m is the perpendicular bisector of PP′ — the same distance away on the far side, along the perpendicular. Nothing travels; the map is the finished pairing of each point with its partner, and doing it twice gives every point back. The textbooks call this map “reflection in m” (British usage) or “reflection across m” (American). The preposition is an idiom naming the line that does the reflecting, as in “your image in the mirror”; it does not describe a direction. On this page the map is called the mirror with line m, or as a shorter name, the mirror in m. The theorem of Cartan and Dieudonné says that every congruence of n-dimensional space is a product of at most n mirrors; in the plane, at most two — three if translations are included in the count, as they are below. So the list of binaries on the sketch (plus/minus, odd/even, left/right versus front/back) is replaced by one object. Everything else on this page is built from it.

2. Sign = count of mirrors, mod 2

Apply mirrors one after another and count them. An even count returns the figure to its own hand; an odd count gives the opposite hand. The parity does not depend on which mirrors were used, only on how many: it is the one invariant every congruence carries without ambiguity. Plus/minus (the determinant, +1 or −1) and odd/even are the same fact in two notations.

Which mirror is not an invariant. Any odd congruence is any chosen mirror followed by some even congruence; the choice of mirror is free, the parity is not. This is the whole of the left/right versus front/back puzzle: a mirror reverses the axis normal to it (front/back). We say “left/right” because we silently compose with a half-turn about the vertical to line ourselves up with the image. The named axis is a choice of even companion; the sign is intrinsic.

3. Measure = separation of a pair of mirrors

Two mirrors give an even congruence, and the even congruence carries a number. Two mirror lines crossing at angle θ compose to a rotation by 2θ about their crossing point. Two parallel mirror lines at distance d compose to a translation by 2d, perpendicular to them. Perpendicular mirror lines (θ = 90°) give the half-turn, the case in the pin sketches.

So measure sits on the even side and comes from the pair; sign comes from the count. A single mirror has a sign and no measure: there is no half a mirror in the plane. The continuous arc in the sketch belongs to the rotation, that is, to the pair.

4. Dimension: the fold

In the plane the mirror is discrete: no continuous path of congruences of the plane leads from the identity to a mirror, because the sign would have to jump. The wavy fold lines in the sketch already say what changes this. In space, a half-turn about the mirror line — a fold — is continuous, carries a measure (the angle turned, up to 180°), and its trace in the plane, what the plane sees when the fold is complete, is exactly the mirror.

The plane therefore cannot tell whether its mirror is the trace of a mirror in space (odd, one mirror) or of a fold (even, two mirrors). The sign of an ambient map is not determined by its trace. Every mirror in n dimensions is a rotation in n + 1 dimensions, restricted; “discrete” means that the arc lives in a dimension the plane does not have.

Chirality is dimension-relative for the same reason. An L in the plane is chiral: no rotation or translation of the plane superposes it on its mirror image. The same L in space is achiral, because a fold through the spare dimension does superpose it. A figure can be chiral in n dimensions only if it spans all n directions; anything spanning fewer can be turned over through a spare one.

5. Probes: a figure detects only what moves it

This is the principle the pin sketches were built on, stated outright. A figure can distinguish two congruences only if their difference moves it. (The difference of maps A and B is B⁻¹A; if that fixes every point of the figure, the figure cannot tell A from B.)

The half-turn about a point P is the product of two perpendicular mirrors through P: the mirror in the vertical line, then the mirror in the horizontal line. Their difference is the horizontal mirror. A pin lying on the horizontal line is fixed point by point by that mirror, so on that pin the half-turn and the vertical mirror are the same map; this is the “perpendicular” block of the sketch, where both pictures came out alike. A pin parallel to the vertical line but off the horizontal one does feel the horizontal mirror, as head-up becoming head-down; that is the “parallel” block, where the pictures differed. A single pin is a vector and has no hand; only a figure that spans the plane (the L) detects the sign under every placement.

6. Three mirrors, one sheet Monge

The Monge sheet — plan over elevation, profile to the right — is three projection planes H, V, P laid flat. Each plane defines one mirror of space. Below, a probe sits in space; push it through any of the three mirrors and watch two kinds of sign change: the side of the point (which side of a plane it lies on) and the sign of the map that has been done so far. They are kept in separate ledgers because they are separate things. A reversed coordinate is drawn on the far side of its trace — the “jump” of the sketch — so an image may land in another view's field; the small gray label beside each image says which view it belongs to.

Each view is blind to its own mirror. observation The mirror in H changes only z, and the plan does not show z. The two views adjacent to H each display z as a depth measured from the trace of H, and each sees the probe jump across that trace. This is the skip-a-plane rule of descriptive geometry read as a statement about a mirror: the coordinate a mirror reverses is exactly the depth that the two adjacent views display, measured from the trace of that mirror's plane.

Side and sign are different −1's. theorem Side belongs to a point; sign belongs to a map. One mirror reverses one side and the sign. Two mirrors reverse two sides and no sign. The sign read from the count agrees with the hand of the frame after every sequence of pushes: Step 2 at work in space.

The eight maps. theorem Whatever order you push in, the map so far is one of eight: the identity, three mirrors, three half-turns (one about each trace), and the point-reflection. Their signs are even, odd, odd, odd, even, even, even, odd.

What this is not. ledger note The equality of depths in two adjacent views is an equality of measures (the same coordinate shown twice), not a product of signs. The three Monge mirrors are all at 90° to one another, so no measure other than 90° ever enters this figure; it is the sign half of the structure, closed on eight elements. The continuous half — pairs of mirrors at other angles — is the subject of the revolution plate.

Glossary

Mathematical terms first, then drawing terms, then terms retired from the sketch.

congruence
A map of the plane (or of space) to itself that preserves every distance. Also called an isometry. Every congruence is a product of mirrors.
mirror
The map of the plane determined by a line m: each point of m stays; each other point P goes to the point P′ for which m is the perpendicular bisector of PP′. In space, the same with a plane in place of the line. Called “reflection in m” or “reflection across m” in the textbooks; here, “the mirror with line m” or, as a name, “the mirror in m”. Its fixed points are exactly m; applied twice it is the identity (an involution); it preserves every distance. The single generator on this page.
“in”, as in mirror in m
A name, not a direction. Inherited from everyday speech about mirrors (“the image in the glass”), where the preposition points at the thing that reflects. Nothing goes into the line; each point goes across it to its partner. “Through” is kept for the push of a probe (a drawing word); “about” is kept for turns.
sign
The parity of the number of mirrors in a congruence: even or odd. Equivalently the determinant, +1 or −1. Invariant: the same for every way of writing the congruence as a product of mirrors.
even / odd
A congruence is even if it is a product of an even number of mirrors (it preserves hand), odd if of an odd number (it reverses hand).
measure
The number carried by an even congruence: the angle of a rotation, the distance of a translation. It is twice the separation of the pair of mirrors that compose to it. A single mirror has no measure.
rotation
Product of two mirrors in lines crossing at angle θ: a turn by 2θ about the crossing point. Even.
half-turn
Rotation by 180°: the product of two perpendicular mirrors. Even. The in-plane operation drawn with an arc in the sketch.
translation
Product of two mirrors in parallel lines at distance d: a shift by 2d perpendicular to the lines. Even.
glide reflection
A mirror followed by a translation along the mirror's own line. Three mirrors; odd; no fixed points.
fold
A half-turn of space about a line lying in the plane. Continuous, even in space, with a measure (the fold angle). Its trace in the plane, when complete, is the mirror in that line.
trace
What a map of space does to the plane, considered only on the plane. Two different maps of space can have the same trace; in particular a mirror in space and a fold have the same trace, so the trace does not determine the sign of the ambient map.
hand
The property of a figure that a mirror reverses and an even congruence preserves. “Opposite hand” means mirror image.
chiral / achiral
A figure is chiral in a given space if no even congruence of that space superposes it on its mirror image; achiral otherwise. Chirality is relative to the space: a figure can be chiral in n dimensions and achiral in n + 1. A figure is chiral in n dimensions only if it spans all n directions.
probe
A figure used to test a map by looking at its image. A probe distinguishes two maps only if the difference of the maps moves the probe. A single pin is a weak probe (it is a vector, with no hand); an L is a complete probe in the plane.
difference of two maps
For maps A and B, the map B⁻¹A. If it fixes a figure point by point, A and B agree on that figure.
fixed point
A point sent to itself by a map. The set of fixed points classifies plane congruences: every point (identity), a line (mirror), one point (rotation), none (translation, glide).
Cartan–Dieudonné theorem
Every congruence of n-dimensional space is a product of at most n mirrors (at most n + 1 when translations are counted, as on this page). The floor under everything here.
projection plane
One of H (horizontal), V (vertical), P (profile): three mutually perpendicular planes in space (Step 6). Each defines a mirror of space.
side
The sign of a point's signed distance from a plane: which side of the plane it lies on. A property of a point, not of a map. A mirror in that plane reverses it.
frame
A point with three short arms in the directions of increasing x, y, z (the signed distances from P, V, H). It has a hand: right if the arms, taken in the order x, y, z, are arranged as at the start of Figure 7; left otherwise. “Right” is the name given to the starting hand.
point-reflection
The product of three mutually perpendicular mirrors of space: every coordinate reversed. Odd.
pin
Drawing term. A segment with a head at one end, drawn as a black stroke with a red disc. It represents an oriented segment: a vector with a position.
head
Drawing term. The red disc marking which end of a pin is which.
L
Drawing term. The figure used as a probe with a hand: a short bar and a long bar meeting at a right angle, unequal so that its mirror image cannot be rotated onto it.
fold line
Drawing term. The drawn line about which a fold is performed; the same line that the resulting mirror fixes.
mirror line
Drawing term. The drawn line of a mirror; the line of its fixed points.
plan / elevation
Drawing terms. Plan: the plane viewed face-on. Elevation: the same plane viewed edge-on, so that a fold is seen as an arc.
trace (of a projection plane)
Drawing term. The line in which two projection planes meet: a = H∩V, b = H∩P, c = V∩P, all through the corner point. To lay the sheet flat, H is turned about a and P about c; these two are hinges and are drawn once. b lies in both turning planes and is carried by both, so it is drawn twice: above the corner (with H) and to the right of it (with P). Hence each drawn line carries two traces: the horizontal line is a on the left and b on the right; the vertical line is b above and c below. Distinct from trace of a map, above.
probe image (plan, elevation, profile)
Drawing term. The drawing of the frame in one view: the point and the two arms that view can show. In Figure 7, x is read leftward from the vertical line (plan and elevation); y upward from a (plan) and rightward from c (profile); z downward from the horizontal line (a in the elevation, b in the profile). A negative value is drawn on the other side of the line.
ground line (not used)
Monge's ligne de terre: the trace H∩V in first-angle projection, where the object stands on H and the line is literally the ground, with the elevation above it. The plates here are plan-over-elevation, in which H lies above the object; there the word misleads, so the line is named by its trace, a.
flip (retired)
Used in the sketch both for the in-plane half-turn and for the out-of-plane fold, which are different maps. Replaced by half-turn and fold.
parallel / perpendicular (re-assigned)
In the sketch these labeled the pin's orientation relative to the axis. They are now used only for the arrangement of a pair of mirror lines (Step 3), which is what they were tracking.
helicity (removed)
A physics term, frame-dependent there, with no clean analog in the plane. The notion wanted was chirality.
left/right, front/back (absorbed)
Not primary binaries; a corollary of Step 2. The mirror reverses front/back; “left/right” names the axis after a chosen even companion is composed in.
