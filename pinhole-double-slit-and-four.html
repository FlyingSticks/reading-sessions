Pinhole, Double Slit and the Number Four

A line in space takes four numbers. A camera fixes two of them in advance and leaves two free. The pinhole and the double slit differ only in which two they fix.

What the plate shows

  • A point costs two numbers; a slit costs one. Requiring a line to pass through a point fixes two of its four numbers. Requiring it to cross a slit fixes one.
  • Both cameras leave two. That is why each produces a two-number family of rays, enough to fill a flat picture and no more.
  • A pinhole is two slits crossed on one card. A slit parallel to ACROSS at up₂ = 0.83 and a slit parallel to UP at across₂ = 0.68, both cut in card 2, cross at C. Their common opening is the pinhole. The double slit is such a pair of cuts placed on two different cards, one cut on each.
  • The free numbers are not the same kind in the two cameras. In the pinhole both free numbers sit on one card and amount to a direction from C. In the double slit one sits on each card, and the rays have no common point.
  • Through a general point of space there is exactly one ray of the pinhole, and exactly one ray of this double slit.

Conventions

  • Card 1 is the card of the previous plate, with the same zero mark, unit of length, ACROSS and UP.
  • Card 2 is parallel to card 1 and 2 units in front of it. Its zero mark O₂ lies on the line through O₁ square to both cards, and its ACROSS and UP are parallel to those of card 1.
  • The slits are straight here. A curved slit, such as a half-circle cut in a sphere, still costs one number, so the count 4 − 1 − 1 = 2 is unchanged. What changes is that the fixed number can no longer be read as a single ruling on a card, and a general point may lie on more than one ray.
  • One exception, as before. A line parallel to the cards pierces neither, and has no numbers in this scheme.
