Three mirrors, one sheet Monge

The Monge sheet — plan over elevation, profile to the right — is three projection planes H, V, P laid flat. Each plane defines one mirror of space. Below, a probe sits in space; push it through any of the three mirrors and watch two kinds of sign change: the side of the point (which side of a plane it lies on) and the sign of the map that has been done so far. They are kept in separate ledgers because they are separate things. A reversed coordinate is drawn on the far side of its trace — the “jump” of the sketch — so an image may land in another view's field; the small gray label beside each image says which view it belongs to.

Each view is blind to its own mirror. observation The mirror in H changes only z, and the plan does not show z. The two views adjacent to H each display z as a depth measured from the trace of H, and each sees the probe jump across that trace. This is the skip-a-plane rule of descriptive geometry read as a statement about a mirror: the coordinate a mirror reverses is exactly the depth that the two adjacent views display, measured from the trace of that mirror's plane.

Side and sign are different −1's. theorem Side belongs to a point; sign belongs to a map. One mirror reverses one side and the sign. Two mirrors reverse two sides and no sign. The sign read from the count agrees with the hand of the frame after every sequence of pushes: step 2 of the One Generator page at work in space.

The eight maps. theorem Whatever order you push in, the map so far is one of eight: the identity, three mirrors, three half-turns (one about each trace), and the point-reflection. Their signs are even, odd, odd, odd, even, even, even, odd.

What this is not. ledger note The equality of depths in two adjacent views is an equality of measures (the same coordinate shown twice), not a product of signs. The three Monge mirrors are all at 90° to one another, so no measure other than 90° ever enters this figure; it is the sign half of the structure, closed on eight elements. The continuous half — pairs of mirrors at other angles — is the subject of the pair plate, plate 3 of the thread. (The revolution plate, side plate B, takes up the fold line of side plate A.)

Glossary for this plate

Terms not listed here are as on the One Generator page.

projection plane
One of H (horizontal), V (vertical), P (profile): three mutually perpendicular planes in space. Each defines a mirror of space.
side
The sign of a point's signed distance from a plane: which side of the plane it lies on. A property of a point, not of a map. A mirror in that plane reverses it.
frame
A point with three short arms in the directions of increasing x, y, z (the signed distances from P, V, H). It has a hand: right if the arms, taken in the order x, y, z, are arranged as at the start of Figure 1; left otherwise. “Right” is the name given to the starting hand.
point-reflection
The product of three mutually perpendicular mirrors of space: every coordinate reversed. Odd.
half-turn
Rotation by 180°: the product of two perpendicular mirrors. Even. The in-plane operation drawn with an arc in the sketch.
trace (of a projection plane)
Drawing term. The line in which two projection planes meet: a = H∩V, b = H∩P, c = V∩P, all through the corner point. To lay the sheet flat, H is turned about a and P about c; these two are hinges and are drawn once. b lies in both turning planes and is carried by both, so it is drawn twice: above the corner (with H) and to the right of it (with P). Hence each drawn line carries two traces: the horizontal line is a on the left and b on the right; the vertical line is b above and c below. Distinct from trace of a map, on side plate A.
probe image (plan, elevation, profile)
Drawing term. The drawing of the frame in one view: the point and the two arms that view can show. In Figure 1, x is read leftward from the vertical line (plan and elevation); y upward from a (plan) and rightward from c (profile); z downward from the horizontal line (a in the elevation, b in the profile). A negative value is drawn on the other side of the line.
ground line (not used)
Monge's ligne de terre: the trace H∩V in first-angle projection, where the object stands on H and the line is literally the ground, with the elevation above it. The plates here are plan-over-elevation, in which H lies above the object; there the word misleads, so the line is named by its trace, a.
