one axis, two pencils — and two readings of each

The folded diagram from before, asked the next question: how do glide constructions and cross-slit projection stand to each other on this drawing? Answer on the sheet: they are the same pencils, run in opposite directions. The camera reading pulls the scene point in through the slit to the screen; the glide construction pushes the footprint out through the same slit to the point's level and carries it home. One reciprocal apart, hinged at the corner mark.

One pencil, two directions — the transposed trace

  
Every element of the answer was already on the folded sheet; it only needed the arrows. The
  camera reading takes the ray from the scene point through the slit and
  reads it where it strikes the screen: Xc = X·m, with
  m = 1/(1 + r·λ). The glide reading takes the
  ray from the footprint through the same slit, reads it at the scene point's own depth, and
  carries the height home along the level plane: Xg = X/m. Two members
  of one pencil through one foot — one traversed inward as optics, one traversed outward as
  T-square work. The gauges are exact reciprocals, product 1 at every depth, which is why the two
  structures blur so easily: they live one reciprocal apart.


  
Same slot, two questions

  
Sharper still: at any level the camera map is f(t) = t·m, and the
  two marks are the same map asked opposite questions. Forward — where does X print? —
  gives Xc = f(X). Backward — what prints at X? — gives
  Xg = f⁻¹(X). The glide construction is the camera run in reverse
  through its own slit. So the three marks stand on the slot in geometric progression,
  Xc : x : Xg with ratio 1/m, the corner
  rig's ⊥ mark holding the middle as mean proportional: Xg·Xc = x²,
  live in the readout. The lower inset draws the identity in the mirror-circle grammar: the
  circle on diameter [Xc, Xg] sees the slot origin with power x², tangent witness attached — and
  the fourth mark −x completes the hinge, (Xg, Xc; x, −x) = −1.


  
Two ladders from one rig

  
Turn the ladders on and hold one depth line (height X, running from the picture plane to the
  point). At equal depth steps the camera marks crowd harmonically —
  reciprocals in arithmetic progression, the graduation welded to foreshortening — while the
  glide marks step out arithmetically along one straight stroke: the
  Reach gauge, equal steps, ∞ unmarked. That is the law-and-gauge split in one picture: same
  armature law through the same foot, but the camera's graduation is forced by optics and glide's
  is a free choice — Reach here; the 1984 book's Turn re-graduates the same straight strokes
  harmonically to seat ∞ at a finite mark. The depth line's two images are the two intervals
  [Xc, x] and [x, Xg], adjacent on the slot, hinged at the corner mark.


  
Where the fold sits between them

  
The fold angle is the dial between the two practices. Open at 90°, the sheet is camera
  country: a physical ray must thread both slits, so the forward reading is honest optics only
  when the two planes stand at their true right angle. Folded flat, it is drafting country: both
  channels lie on one sheet, and the glide construction — out through the foot, level back home —
  is stroke work that never leaves the paper. The picture plane reads ×1 under both: window to
  the camera, where marks land; floor to the glide, level 0 where marks stand. And the seam meter
  Δ = r_u·r_v·(r_v − r_u) in the readout is the rig's own referee — drag v onto u, or send a foot
  toward −∞, and the badge flips to SEAM as the two readings' drawn lines fuse.
