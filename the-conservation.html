The Conservation

The area identity from the sketch sheet — adjacent, overlapping, and the account that ignores its arrangement

This page makes exact the top-left panel of the 11 September sketch sheet: the sum of overlapping areas equals the sum of adjacent areas, with two patches marked in yellow and the word defect beside each. It is the instrument the defect page names but never draws. That page gives the defect three properties — provable, conserved, quantitative — and shows the first and third at the closure test; for the second it offers only the carpet: flatten the bump here and it rises there, the mismatch fixed before you knelt down. The sketch's panel proposes the measure the carpet lacks. Cut one figure into strips two ways and weigh them: the difference is an area, it sits in nameable cells, and no rearrangement changes its total.

The figure is the sketch's own grid. A constant count runs down the side — levels 0 through 4, step h = 1, the ordinal skeleton. A placement runs along the top — ticks b0 … b4, ends pinned at 0 and the span S, the interior free: smear them, pile them, grade them toward a seat. Each level owns a horizontal strip, each placement gap a vertical one. Everything then turns on one small question the hand hides and the algebra cannot: where does each strip stop? Anchor the horizontal strips one tick short of their level's own gap and the two families tile the rectangle exactly — that is the adjacent arrangement. Let each run to its own tick and the families double-pay the diagonal — overlapping. Hold both families short and the diagonal is missed entirely — starved. The diagonal is a staircase of corner cells, one per level, each h wide by one gap deep: the sketch's yellow squares, drawn crimson here by palette discipline.

The area identity

For a constant count of step h against any monotone placement b0 < … < bN:

  1. the adjacent strips tile the rectangle: their sum is N·S, no gap, no double-cover;
  2. the overlapping strips exceed it by the corner cells: overlapping = adjacent + A;
  3. the starved strips miss the same cells: starved = adjacent − A;
  4. the account is A = h · (bN − b0) — one count-step of span, blind to every interior choice.

Checked in check-the-area-identity.js, 33/33, 11 September 2026. The anchoring conventions are the auditor's reading of the hand sketch; the instrument computes all three side by side, so a different cut of the strips argues with it rather than replaces it.

The fourth line is the conservation. The account does not merely resist rearrangement — it never sees the arrangement at all. Drag the interior ticks below, or jump between the three policies, and watch the strip sums swing while the crimson total holds at one count-step of span: A = 1 × 187.5. The corner cells relocate — four equal cells under smear, one swollen cell under pile, a graded run shrinking toward the seat — and their sum is bit-identical in every case. The bill was fixed before you knelt down; here is the ledger that proves it.

Drag the gold ticks (or focus one and use the arrow keys) — the interior placement is yours; the ends are pinned. Teal, one payment from a level's strip; purple, one from a gap's strip; crimson, paid twice; dashed, paid never. Adjacent tiles for every placement you can reach. Overlapping always exceeds by the crimson exactly; starved always falls short by the same figure. The account line in the readout is the number that refuses to move.

The joint, priced

The defect page ends its count-and-placement section with a sentence this identity turns into arithmetic: the defect is born at the joint between the two. A corner cell is that joint — the one place a level's strip and its own gap's strip meet, h of count wide, one gap of placement deep. Sum the joints and the placement telescopes away; only the ends survive. That is why the account reads h × span: the count contributes its step, the placement contributes only its extremities, and everything a policy can decide — where the gaps sit, which joints are fat — cancels. Smear, pile and grade are placements of the bill, not sizes of it, which is the sketch's smear-pile-grade panel and the carpet in one figure.

And the count keeps its other promise: it is the only party that can starve the bill. Press a finer count against the same span — halve the step, halve every corner cell's width — and the account halves with it, exactly, linearly. No interior cleverness required, none possible. Only the completed count zeroes it, and a completed count is the Turn side of Theorem 0: you may refine forever, or you may finish; the instrument below refines.

The graded placement, refined. Each halving of the count step doubles the corner cells and halves each one's width; the bill bar drops 187.5 → 93.75 → 46.88 → 23.44, ratio 2.0000 at every stage. The defect page's sentence — counting is how the law's refusal becomes a number — acquires its rate: the number is one count-step's worth of span, and only the step can spend it down.

Two defects, two spaces

A discipline note, so the panels stay consistent. This page's account is written A, not D — the rig reserves D for the slit separation (D = 100) — and A is not the book's cubic. The cover's split-measure Δ = rurv(rv − ru) lives in rate space and prices the disagreement between the two readings of one congruence; A lives in count-versus-placement space and prices the disagreement between two anchorings of one gauge. Session 4 already caught this species of confusion once — the miniature's crossing is not the seam — and the same care applies here. What the two measures share is the grammar, which is the point of the sheet: a total fixed by the law before any choice, a policy that relocates it, and zero available only on a locus, never by arrangement.
