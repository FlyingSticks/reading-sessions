The involution

What the mirror is made of. Version 1, 11 November 2026.

On the earlier plates the mirror is the generator: every congruence of the plane is a product of mirrors, the count of mirrors gives the sign, and the pair gives the measure. This plate goes one level below the mirror and asks what the mirror itself is made of. The answer uses only two things: a pairing of points that undoes itself, and the harmonic relation, the value −1.

Figure 1 draws the pairing with nothing at infinity, so that every part of it is on the page. Figure 2 sends one point of Figure 1 off to infinity and the familiar mirror appears. Figure 3 keeps that point at infinity and turns its direction, and the three mirrors of the three-pairs plate appear as three settings of one dial. A list of what is claimed, and a glossary, follow. Drawing words and mathematical words are kept apart.

1. The pairing

An involution is a rule that pairs points: each point X has one partner X′, and the partner of X′ is X again. Applying the rule twice returns every point to where it started. A point that is its own partner is a fixed point.

The pairing of this plate is built from two given things: a line m and a point C that is not on m. To find the partner of a point X:

  1. Draw the straight line through C and X. It crosses m at a point; call it F.
  2. The partner X′ is the point on that same line for which the four points C, F, X, X′ are harmonic: their cross-ratio is −1. (The glossary gives the formula.)

This plate calls it the harmonic pairing with line m and centre C.

In Figure 1, drag X or C. Three things to try. Put X on m: its partner is itself. Put X on C: its partner is itself. Put X where X′ was: the partner lands where X was.

The straightedge construction

The partner can be found with a straightedge alone, with no measuring. Choose any point P off the line CF, and any point Q on the line PX. Join C to Q; that line crosses PF at R. Join F to Q; that line crosses PC at S. The line through R and S crosses the line CF at X′. The four points P, Q, R, S are a complete quadrangle, the figure of your incidence sheet. So the pairing is stated by incidence only, and −1 is the one value incidence can state. The button draws this, and the ledger checks that it lands on the same X′.

Why the value is −1

Suppose a rule keeps straight lines straight, fixes every point of m, fixes C, and is an involution. Then the value −1 is forced. The reason, in four steps:

  1. The line through C and X contains two fixed points, C and F. A straight line is settled by two of its points, so the rule sends this line to itself. Hence X′ is on it.
  2. A rule that keeps straight lines straight keeps the cross-ratio of four points on a line. (This is a standard theorem, used here and not proved here.) So the cross-ratio of C, F, X, X′ equals the cross-ratio of their partners C, F, X′, X.
  3. Exchanging the last two points of a cross-ratio turns its value r into 1 ÷ r. So r = 1 ÷ r, that is, r² = 1.
  4. The equation r² = 1 has two roots. The root +1 happens only when X and X′ are the same point, which is the rule that does nothing. The other root is −1.

Step 4 is the fold page again: one square, two roots. The root +1 is the pairing that leaves every point alone. The root −1 is the pairing of this plate.

2. The centre leaves the page

The pairing of Figure 1 is not a congruence. It keeps straight lines straight, but it does not keep lengths. Figure 2 shows this with the probe of the earlier plates, a letter L, and then shows what removes the distortion.

The slider moves C away from m along the line through 0 perpendicular to m. At the right-hand end of the slider C is at infinity: the grey lines, which all passed through C, are then parallel, and each is perpendicular to m.

Two things happen together at that end. First, the harmonic relation becomes the midpoint rule: F is the midpoint of X and X′. Second, the dashed line h leaves the page. The line h is the set of points whose partners are at infinity; it lies halfway between m and C. While h is on the page, parallel lines have partners that converge, and lengths are not kept. When C is at infinity, so is h: points at infinity are then paired with points at infinity, and the pairing is the mirror with line m.

A draftsman will recognise the change in the grey lines: converging at a station point, then parallel. That is offered as a likeness, not as a claim.

3. The direction of the centre

In Figure 3 the centre C stays at infinity. A point at infinity is a direction, so all that is left of C is a dial. Whatever the dial reads, the pairing is still an involution, each point of m is still fixed, and F is still the midpoint of X and X′. What changes is which lengths, if any, are kept.

The three-pairs plate named this direction the across direction, and gave one drawing rule for it: the across direction is the direction of the tangent to the unit locus at the point where m crosses it. Each decree has its own unit locus, so each decree sets the dial differently. The three buttons set the dial by that rule. The ledger then measures all three forms at the corner X and at its partner X′, and reports which form is kept.

So a decree does one job here. The line m is given. The pairing is harmonic in every case. The decree only says which point at infinity is the centre that goes with m.

direction of C

As on the three-pairs plate, the parabolic and Minkowski mirrors keep their own plane's form but not ruler distance on the page. Only the line m drawn here is treated; it passes through 0 and is flatter than the diagonal, so all three decrees accept it as a mirror line.

5. Glossary

Mathematical words

Involution
A rule that sends each point to a partner, such that applying the rule twice returns every point. A mirror is an involution. So is a half-turn. This plate treats the kind that fixes every point of one line.
Partner
The point that a pairing assigns to a given point. The partner of X is written X′.
Fixed point
A point that is its own partner.
Cross-ratio
A number belonging to four points on one line, taken in order. Measure positions c, f, x, x′ along the line from any starting point. The cross-ratio of C, F, X, X′ is ((x − c) × (x′ − f)) ÷ ((x′ − c) × (x − f)). It does not depend on the starting point or on the unit of length.
Harmonic
Four points on a line are harmonic when their cross-ratio is −1.
Harmonic pairing with line m and centre C
The pairing defined in section 1. This is the plate's own name. The standard name is harmonic homology with axis m and centre C.
Point at infinity
The point where a family of parallel lines is said to meet. Each direction has exactly one. It has no dot on the drawing; it shows only as lines being parallel.
The line h
The set of points whose partners are at infinity. It is parallel to m and halfway between m and C.
Across direction, unit locus, decree, form
As on the three-pairs plate. The three forms, with x measured across the page from 0 and y up the page from 0: Euclidean x² + y², parabolic x², Minkowski x² − y². The unit locus is where the form equals 1.
Mirror with line m
As on the earlier plates. On this plate it is found to be the harmonic pairing with line m whose centre is the point at infinity in the across direction.

Drawing words

Filled dot
A solid disc marking a given point.
Ringed dot
A small open circle marking a point that is found, not given. “Ringed” is a drawing word only.
Probe
The black letter L. It has a corner X, a long stroke and a short stroke.
Dashed, dotted
Dashed: the line h, and in Figure 1 the line through R and S. Dotted: a direction line through 0.
