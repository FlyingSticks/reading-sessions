Two meanings of "congruence"

The book and the plates use the same word for two unrelated things. Both come from one Latin root, congruere, "to come together, to agree". Neither is a special case of the other.

  • Line congruence (the book): a set of lines in space.
  • Congruence map (the plates): a map of the plane that keeps every distance.

Diagram 1 · the book's senseA line congruence is a set of lines

  • Picking out one line among all the lines of space takes four numbers. A line congruence is a family of lines in which two numbers are enough.
  • The book's case: choose a point on slit a (one number) and a point on slit b (one number), and join them. Every line of the family arises this way.
  • The thin green lines are 25 members of the family. The thick green line is the one member the two sliders select.

Nothing here is a map: no point is sent anywhere. And no distance is used: the definition says only "meets slit a and meets slit b". The stretch slider shows this. A stretch changes lengths, yet the stretched family is still exactly the lines meeting the two stretched slits.

The name is from 19th-century line geometry (Plücker). As far as I know, the "agreement" is that these are the lines two larger families have in common: the lines meeting slit a, and the lines meeting slit b. I have not checked that reading against Plücker's text.

Diagram 2 · the plates' senseA congruence map keeps every distance

  • This sense descends from Euclid: two figures are congruent when one can be laid on the other so that they agree everywhere.
  • The plates give the name to the map that does the laying-on: a map of the plane that keeps every distance. The thread's claim is that every such map is a product of mirrors.
  • The dark figure is the original. The blue figure is its image under the map. P and Q are two marked points of the figure; P′ and Q′ are their images.

Here distance is the whole content, and the thing named is a map, not a collection. The stretch is shown for contrast: it is a map of the plane too, but it changes distances, so it is not a congruence map.

Diagram 3 · the one bridgeThe map σ that belongs to the two slits

  • Through any point P off the slits runs exactly one line of the family. It meets slit a at A and slit b at B.
  • σ pairs P with the point P′ on that same line for which (P, P′; A, B) = −1. Applied twice, σ returns every point.
  • The lines σ sends onto themselves are exactly the book's line family. So the line congruence belongs to one map.
  • Is that map a congruence map in the plates' sense? Compare the length PQ with the length P′Q′, and push slit b away.

With slit b at a finite depth, σ changes distances, so it is not a congruence map. With slit b at infinity and perpendicular to slit a, σ is the half-turn about slit a: two perpendicular mirror planes that both contain slit a. That one case is where the two senses of the word meet. In general, the book's line congruence belongs to a map that is not a congruence in the plates' sense.

Words used on this page

slit a
the straight line x = 0 at depth z = 150. It runs up and down (the y direction).
slit b
the straight line y = 0 at depth z = 250 unless a slider changes that depth. It runs left and right (the x direction).
frame
a drawn rectangle at one depth, only to show where that depth is.
line family
all the lines that meet both slits. This is the book's line congruence.
map
a rule that sends each point to a point.
image
where a map sends a point or a figure.
mirror with line m
the map of the plane that pairs points across the line m, with m the perpendicular bisector of each pair.
product of mirrors
one mirror applied, then another applied to the result.
sign
+1 for an even number of mirrors, −1 for an odd number.
stretch
a map that multiplies distances in one direction by a factor and leaves the perpendicular direction alone.
σ
the map of space defined under Diagram 3.
half-turn about a line
the map of space that turns every point 180° about that line.
