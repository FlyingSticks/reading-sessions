The pair

Two mirrors at a general angle — where measure enters. Plate v1. Revised 11 November 2026: placed in the thread (line below); one forward reference added. A “What is claimed” table added.

A single mirror has a sign and no measure. Two mirrors have a measure: the angle between their lines. The product of the two is a turn through twice that angle, and it remembers the angle and nothing else about the pair.

Everything on the Monge plates happened at 90°, where two mirrors compose to a half-turn and the order of the two does not matter. This plate unlocks the angle. Two mirror lines A and B cross at the point O at angle φ. The probe is the L. The first dial sets φ. The second dial turns the pair of lines together about O, keeping φ fixed. The switch reverses the order in which the two mirrors are applied.

What the plate shows

1. The product is a turn through 2φ about O. theorem Measure directions from O by their angle. A mirror whose line makes angle β with the reference sends a direction at angle θ to the direction at angle 2β − θ (the line is the bisector of the two). Take A as the reference, β = 0, and B at β = φ. Then A sends θ to −θ, and B sends −θ to 2φ + θ. Net: θ to θ + 2φ, for every θ. Every direction has been turned through 2φ, and O stays put: that is the turn through 2φ about O. Drag φ to 90° and the turn is 180°, the half-turn of the pin sketches and of the Monge plates. Drag φ to 0° and the two mirrors coincide and cancel: the turn is 0°, the identity.

Where does the doubling come from? Not from a formula. It comes from one property of a mirror line — it is the bisector between every point and its partner — used on a well-chosen probe: the mirror line A itself. Figure 2 builds the argument in the three steps of the sketch of 9 Nov, with a fourth as a check.

1a. The argument, step by step. theorem Step 1. One mirror line, A. It fixes every point on itself. Step 2. A second mirror line B through the same point O, at separation θ. Step 3. Send A across B. Take any point X of A; its partner X′ lies on the perpendicular from X to B, the same distance beyond (the right-angle marks). Two such partners fix the image line A′. Since B is the perpendicular bisector of every segment XX′, B bisects the angle between A and A′: θ on one side, θ on the other, so A to A′ is 2θ. That is the doubling, and it is only the bisecting property, used once. Now read it as the product “A then B”: the first mirror leaves its own line alone, so on this probe only the second mirror acts, and it carries A to A′. A map fixing O that swings a line through O by 2θ is the turn through 2θ about O. Step 4. Choose B as the probe instead. The first mirror carries B to B″, at θ on the far side of A, which is 2θ from B. The second carries B″, at −2θ from B, to +2θ from B. B has been turned through 2θ: the same answer from a different probe.

2. The product remembers only the separation. theorem Turn the pair with the second dial. The two mirror lines swing about O, the grey intermediate image swings with them, and the red image does not move. The argument of item 1 used only φ, never the absolute position of A: so any pair of mirrors through O with separation φ gives the same turn. The pair is not recoverable from the product. What survives is the measure, 2φ, and the point O.

3. Order matters, except at 0° and 90°. theorem A then B turns through +2φ; B then A turns through −2φ (run item 1 with the roles exchanged). The two agree only when +2φ and −2φ are the same turn, that is when 4φ is a multiple of 360°: φ = 0° or φ = 90°. At 90° both orders give the half-turn, which is why the three Monge mirrors could be pushed in any order on side plate E, Three mirrors, one sheet. Away from 90° the purple image sits elsewhere — the A·B versus B·A of the first sketch.

4. Sign and measure, side by side. observation Whatever the dials say, the red L is the same hand as the black one: two mirrors, even. The sign never moves. The measure moves continuously with φ. One mirror cannot be turned a little; a pair can. This is the thesis of the mirrors page with the dial in hand.

What this plate is not. ledger note The two lines cross. Two parallel mirror lines compose to a shift, with measure a distance rather than an angle; that case is on the mirrors page, Figure 3, and is the subject of the next plate, the parallel pair. Nor does this plate leave the plane: the fold of the revolution plate is a half-turn of space, a different map whose trace in the plane is a single mirror.

Glossary for this plate

mirror
The map of the plane determined by a line m: each point of m stays; each other point P goes to the point P′ for which m is the perpendicular bisector of PP′ — the same distance away on the far side, along the perpendicular. Called “reflection in m” or “reflection across m” in the textbooks; here “the mirror with line m”, or as a name “the mirror in m”. The “in” names the line, as in “the image in the glass”; it is not a direction. Odd.
mirror line drawing word
The drawn line of a mirror; the line of its fixed points. Two on this plate, A (teal) and B (gold).
pair
Two mirrors whose lines cross at a point O. Its separation is the angle φ between the lines, taken from A to B, 0° ≤ φ ≤ 90°.
product
One mirror followed by the other. Written in words — “A then B” — because the dot notation is read in both directions in the literature.
turn (rotation)
The map that fixes one point O and turns every direction through the same angle about it. Even. The product of a pair with separation φ is the turn through 2φ about O. The half-turn is the case 2φ = 180°.
measure
The number carried by the product of a pair: here the angle 2φ. A single mirror has no measure. The measure is an invariant of the product; the pair that produced it is not.
sign
Even or odd, by the count of mirrors. The product of a pair is always even, whatever φ.
bisector
A mirror line m is the perpendicular bisector of the segment joining any point to its partner: it meets the segment at right angles and at its midpoint. Hence m bisects the angle between any line through a point of m and that line's image. This single property is the source of the doubling.
order
Which of the two mirrors is applied first. Changing the order changes the turn from +2φ to −2φ. Two maps for which order does not matter are said to commute; a pair commutes only at φ = 0° and φ = 90°.
image line drawing word
In Figure 2, the line A′ (or B″, B‴) drawn red: where a mirror line lands when sent across the other. Two partners determine it.
the L drawing word
The probe: a short bar and a long bar at a right angle, unequal so that it has a hand. Drawn black; its image after one mirror grey; after both red; in the other order purple.
mark drawing word
A thin dotted outline recording where the red image stood when the pair dial was at 90°. The red image stays inside it as the pair is turned; that is item 2 made visible.
