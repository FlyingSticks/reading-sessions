The Miniature

A chart from 1979, the isometric pair, and the pattern with its defect held constant

The author's first book, Graphics for Architecture, opened on a premise: every architectural graphic — plan, elevation, section, axonometric, oblique, perspective — can be generated two ways. Built with rules, step by step, constructed on the plane; or thrown by projection, out of descriptive geometry. Its classification chart set efecto pictórico down the left margin and sistema de proyección down the right, with a single column of pictures between them — the same images read from the left as effects, from the right as systems. The page's own layout says it: one column, two readings, meeting in the middle.

In the vocabulary this record has been building, the premise maps almost term for term. Drawing built with rules: the law is fixed before any object arrives — verticals stay vertical, receding edges run at 30°, parallels stay parallel — and what the draughtsman keeps is the gauge, marking the receding edge in true units at whatever scale he likes. The floor's arrangement: fixed law, free gauge. Projection: the apparatus is chosen freely — ray direction, plane attitude — but once chosen, the geometry hands over the graduation, and no one gets to adjust it. The window's arrangement: free law, welded gauge.

The pair on the sheet

The book's own miniature of its premise was the isometric pair: one cube, drawn and projected. The isometric drawing constructs the cube on the plane at full size — unit edges drawn one unit long, at 30° left and right. The isometric projection throws the same cube along its own diagonal onto a plane, and every edge lands foreshortened by the same welded factor. The two figures are the same figure at two sizes. One picture, two parents.

The welded factor deserves its honest record, because it is where this page began. The author's sheet annotated the pair with .866, pulled from memory across four decades. Audited, the edge foreshortening is √(2/3) ≈ .8165. But .866 is not a fiction — it is cos 30°, and it lives in the same construction: it is the horizontal run of a unit receding edge on the drawing, the width you would measure across the paper. Two real constants of one figure; memory reached into the right drawing and pulled out the neighboring number. The correction was accepted, and the audit went one step further than either constant: the drawing map and the projection map are not merely similar — they are the same map up to one scale factor, D = P / √(2/3), checked over every one of the cube's twenty-eight vertex pairs. The chart's two columns produce one congruence, in the strictest sense the word has.

One cube, two parents. Left, the drawing: the law is fixed — verticals vertical, 30° and 30° — and only the gauge answers to you; slide the unit and the figure breathes. Right, the projection: the apparatus is yours — spin it where you like — but the gauge is welded; nothing you do touches .8165. Overlay them and the whole disagreement collapses to a single ratio. Set the unit to .8165 and it is not a disagreement at all.

The defect held constant

Why is the disagreement one number? Because parallel projection is a linear map, and so is a rule-built drawing: neither can treat one part of space differently from another. The ratio between the two figures cannot depend on where the cube sits — move it anywhere and nothing changes (the check script marches it around; nothing changes). So the defect between the two gauges is constant at every depth, and a constant defect is a removable one: a single slide of the unit — redraw at .8165 — zeroes it everywhere at once. That is the normalization a century of draughtsmen performed by shrug. The distinction between drawing and projection could be treated as pedantry precisely because, in the parallel world, the defect never varies enough to charge for itself.

In the vocabulary of the defect page: the 1979 pair is the pattern with the bill set to a flat rate. The carpet has a bump, but the bump is the same height in every room, so one cut of the carpet fits the whole house. The book's case begins when the rate stops being flat.

Un-parallelling the projection, in the book's Chapter 2 convention (image coordinate x/(1+rλ)). At r = 0 the camera's local scale is .8165 at every depth: the defect against the drawing gauge is one flat number, and the unit slider can zero it everywhere — the 1979 world. Raise the rate and the camera's scale becomes a function of depth: the flat line bends into a curve, and the unit slider can now do only one thing — relocate the curve's zero. Draw smaller than .8165 and there is exactly one depth where the two gauges agree, marked in green; draw at full size and there is none, because a camera that only shrinks never climbs back to meet you. A constant defect is removable; a varying one is only movable. The whole book lives in that difference.

What the miniature is evidence for

Two things, and they should be kept apart. The first is a distinction. In 1979 the two columns were alternatives: a picture was generated one way or the other, and the chart classified it by parentage. The present book's claim is stronger — both readings live on the same picture at once, and the theorem concerns their agreement. First book: every picture has one of two parents. This book: every picture on the seam has both. The miniature is the hinge between those claims, because its two parents produce figures so nearly identical that the classification by parentage was already, quietly, a statement of congruence.

The second is a witness. The porch names building as its fourth case and holds that a porch case must be checkable against a life. This chart is that check, run against the author's own: the quarrel between rules and rays was already a two-column table in his first book, forty-seven years before the pattern had a name. Whether that fact earns a sentence in the book — the porch's building line, or Chapter 1 — is not decided here. It sits in the record and seasons.
