The pair plate found that two mirrors at angle φ make a turn by 2φ. The pair at φ and the pair at φ + 180° are the same two lines and make the same turn. So the product keeps the doubled number 2φ and nothing finer, while the pair of lines holds something finer that the product has lost. This plate writes the mirror and the pair in the arithmetic of each plane, the arithmetic of the fold page, and finds that the doubling is a squaring, and that what the product has lost is the choice between two square roots. Section 5 then tests a tempting claim about the square of g.

1. The arithmetic used

As on the fold page and the three-pairs plate, a point of the plane is a number z = x + g y, with x measured across the page from 0 and y up the page from 0. The three planes differ only in the square of g: g² = −1 (Euclidean), g² = 0 (parabolic), g² = +1 (Minkowski). Numbers are multiplied by the ordinary rules, replacing g² by its value.

Three more words, all in the glossary. The bar of z is z̄ = x − g y; on the page it is the partner of z under the mirror with the horizontal axis as its line. The form of z is z × z̄, which is the form of the three-pairs plate: x² + y², x², or x² − y². The unit locus is the set of numbers whose form is 1. For a number a on the unit locus, a × ā = 1, so dividing by a is the same as multiplying by ā.

A mirror line m through 0 crosses the unit locus at two points, b and −b. Either one names the line.

2. One mirror, in numbers

Statement. The mirror with line m, where m passes through 0 and the unit point b, sends each number z to

z′ = b² × z̄.

Reason. The three-pairs plate defines the mirror with line m by four properties. Four short computations show that this rule has all four. Each uses only b × b̄ = 1 and two facts about any number v: v + v̄ is an ordinary number (it has no g part), and v − v̄ is an ordinary number times g.

  1. It keeps every point of m where it is. A point of m is t × b with t an ordinary number. The rule sends it to b² × t × b̄ = t × b × (b × b̄) = t × b.
  2. The midpoint of z and z′ lies on m. z + z′ = z + b² × z̄ = b × (b̄ × z + b × z̄). The bracket is a number plus its own bar, so it is an ordinary number. So z + z′ is an ordinary number times b: a point of m. Half of it, the midpoint, is a point of m too.
  3. The segment from z to z′ lies in the across direction. z′ − z = b² × z̄ − z = b × (b × z̄ − b̄ × z). The bracket is a number minus its own bar, so it is an ordinary number times g. So z′ − z is an ordinary number times g × b, and the line of g × b is the across direction of m (three-pairs plate, glossary).
  4. It keeps the form. The form of a product is the product of the forms; the form of b is 1; and z̄ has the same form as z.

So the rule is the mirror with line m, by that definition, in all three planes. Nothing is assumed. One consequence, used below: applied twice, the rule does nothing, because the bar of b² × z̄ is b̄² × z, and b² × b̄² × z = (b × b̄)² × z = z.

What to notice. The unit point b enters only as b². Since (−b)² = b², the points b and −b give the same rule, as they must: they name the same line. The line is ±b. The mirror is b².

3. The pair, in numbers

Let line A have unit point a and line B have unit point b. Apply section 2 twice. A sends z to a² × z̄. Then B sends that to b² × (ā² × z). Collect the terms:

A then B sends z to q² × z, where q = b × ā = b ÷ a.

Three things follow.

  • The bar has gone. One mirror has one bar; two mirrors have none. The bar is the sign of the earlier plates: present for an odd product, absent for an even one.
  • The doubling is a squaring. The number q lies on the unit locus, and its measure is the measure from A to B. The congruence multiplies by q², whose measure is twice that.
  • The pair knows q only up to sign. Replace b by −b: the same line B, but q becomes −q. The congruence, q², does not change. So a pair of lines gives two numbers, q and −q, and the congruence gives one, their common square. These are the two roots of the fold page. In the Minkowski plane q² has two more square roots, j × q and −j × q. Their form is −1, so they are not on the unit locus and are not the q of any pair of lines on this plate. From here on, “the two roots” means the two roots on the unit locus.

4. Where the two roots can be told apart

Set Figure 2 to Euclidean and move the slider through 180°. The line B returns to where it was. The coloured L″ returns to where it was. But q has travelled half-way round the circle and now stands where −q stood. The walk was continuous and nothing jumped. So in the Euclidean plane there is no way to say which of q, −q belongs to a pair of lines: either can be walked into the other while the lines end where they began.

Set it to Parabolic or Minkowski. The unit locus now has two separate pieces. The slider moves q along one piece and can never carry it to the other; only the button, which is a jump, puts −q in use. In these two planes the root can be chosen once and for all: take the one on the piece that contains 1.

“Separate” is meant in the plane of numbers. If points at infinity are added to the page, the two pieces meet there, in the directions of the null locus, which are the self-paired directions of plate 10, the absolute: the ε-axis in the parabolic plane, the two diagonals in the Minkowski plane. But a point at infinity is not a number, and no q can stand there.

This is where the square of g acts. With g² = −1 the unit locus is one closed loop, and the two roots are joined. With g² = 0 or +1 it is two pieces, and the two roots are separated.

5. A tempting claim, tested

Each plane is labelled by the square of g: −1, 0 or +1. So it is tempting to say: “each mirror has a square, which is +1, 0 or −1 according to the plane, and to multiply mirrors is literally to multiply.” Tested against sections 2 to 4, the sentence divides into three parts.

PartVerdict
“Multiply the mirrors” is literal multiplication.Holds. The pair is the number q = b ÷ a; the congruence is multiplication by q². Sections 2 and 3.
The square of a mirror is +1, 0 or −1, matching g².Struck. A mirror applied twice does nothing in all three planes; in numbers, b × b̄ = 1 in all three. So a mirror's square is +1 whatever the plane, while g² changes with the plane. In the Minkowski plane the two happen to be the same number, +1; that is a coincidence of value, and the mirror's square does not come from g² there either.
The square of g has a part in the doubling.Holds, differently. It decides whether the two roots q, −q can be walked into each other. Section 4.

There is a standard algebra (Clifford's) in which each mirror line through 0 is itself an element that can be multiplied, the product of two such elements is the number q or its bar, and the square of the element is the form at that point of the line. It says the same as sections 2 and 3 in another notation. It is not developed here.

The last row follows from the first two, because the unit locus is closed under multiplying and under the bar. The bar is the sign. The point w is the measure. What the rule does not hold is the root of w: that belongs to the lines, not to the congruence.

8. Glossary

Mathematical words

Number z = x + g y
A point of the plane, written so that it can be multiplied. g² is −1, 0 or +1 according to the plane.
Bar, z̄
x − g y. The standard name is the conjugate.
Form of z
z × z̄ = x² − g² y². The same form as on the three-pairs plate.
Unit locus
The numbers whose form is 1. Euclidean: a circle. Parabolic: the two vertical lines x = 1 and x = −1. Minkowski: the two branches of a hyperbola.
Unit point of a line
A point where a line through 0 crosses the unit locus. Each mirror line of this plate has two, b and −b.
Measure
Of a point of the unit locus. As on the three-pairs plate: twice the area of the sector from 1 to the point, counted along the unit locus. In the Euclidean plane it is the angle, shown in degrees. In the other two planes it is shown as a plain number, and it is defined only on the piece of the unit locus that contains 1.
q
b ÷ a, which equals b × ā. The number that belongs to the pair A, B once unit points a, b are chosen.
Root
A number whose square is a given number. q and −q are the two roots of q² that lie on the unit locus. In the Minkowski plane q² has two further roots, j × q and −j × q, which do not.
Piece (of the unit locus)
A part that can be walked along without leaving the locus and without a jump.

Drawing words

Filled dot
A point that is given or in use.
Plane colours
Purple for the Euclidean plane, grey for the parabolic, green for the Minkowski, as on the fold page and the three-pairs plate. On this plate the unit locus, the dots z, z′, q, −q and the figure L″ take the plane's colour.
Ringed dot
A small open circle marking a point that is found. “Ringed” is a drawing word only.
Probe
The black letter L of Figure 2.
Drawing rule
The rule of the involution plate for finding a partner: the mirror line passes through the midpoint, and the segment runs in the across direction. It uses no arithmetic of the plane's numbers.
