The cover sentence, the porch, and the plane's two jobs

Read this session: the cover's claim, and the ground under Chapter 1

Landed in the book The cover's glide clause

The cover said the glide draftsman's depth lines were "graduated in equal steps," citing the 1984 book. The reading caught it: the 1984 setups typically graduated their straight depth lines harmonically, vanishing at a finite point along the line. The error was a conflation the book's own Chapter 2 exists to forbid — attributing a gauge (Reach) to a practice whose identity is its law (straightness), and citing as evidence a book that used the other gauge (Turn). Repaired the same day in the cover passage, Chapter 1 §1.1, and the Glide definition in its three copies. The lesson worth keeping: the book's first reader-found error was exactly the mistake the book was written to make impossible, made by the book about itself.

Landed as a page The porch

The reading of the cover produced a want: a lead-in for readers who arrive carrying preconceptions about the picture plane — which is every reader from painting, building, or timekeeping, if not every mathematician. Built as a page outside the numbered spine: three settled quarrels (the canvas as window or wall; the calendar's leftover quarter-day; the map's forced choice of angles or areas), each a law nobody disputed, a graduation somebody had to choose, an impossibility forcing the choice, and a public argument that mistook the gauge for the essence. The middle case took three tries — temperament fell because the author could not vouch for it, relativity because it demanded too much background — and the calendar held because everyone alive has lived inside it. That selection rule is itself a yield: a porch case must be checkable against a life, not against a library.

Rig fact, checked The floor and the window

The session's first observation: the two readings assign the picture plane two different roles, and the split has been argued for centuries in another field's vocabulary. The camera reading takes the plane as Alberti's window — a mark is where a sight line lands. The glide reading takes the same plane as a builder's floor — a mark is a footprint, level 0, the place a straight depth line stands. Marks land; marks stand. Painting's window-versus-surface quarrel (Alberti to Greenberg to Johns) ran on the assumption that these two roles exhaust the plane; the floor is the third role optics never offered, and the glide apparatus has behaved this way all along — depth lines anchored at footprints, graduation calibrated from level 0, the picture-plane line the one line immune to the glide flow. This yield is already load-bearing in the porch's threshold section.

Seasoning Datum both ways

The session's second observation, minutes old and deliberately not yet in the book. The plane is not a datum under one reading and something else under the other: it qualifies as a datum under both — chosen, exempt from its own measure, load-bearing without appearing in the results — and the readings disagree only about what it is a datum for. Under the glide reading it is the datum of depth: the surface the slab is measured from. Under the camera reading the datum of depth sits elsewhere (the slits), and the plane is instead the datum of scale: the level where magnification is exactly 1, the register marks land on. The rig fact underneath is checked and trivially so: at z = 0 the two graduations each read 1 separately — not merely their product, which is 1 everywhere — so the picture plane is the shared unit surface of both gauges, which is precisely what qualifies it to serve as the exempt reference for both readings at once. Alberti's velo concedes the point from inside the window tradition: the moment threads are stretched across the window to draw by, the window has been admitted to be a reference surface.

Held back on purpose. The rig fact would survive any audit; the coinage — "datum of depth" versus "datum of scale" — has not yet been tested against the chapters (whether "datum" is free of other burdens there, whether the phrase survives Chapter 6's codec language, whether the cut still looks right in a month). If it stands, its seat is the porch's threshold section, in one sentence. If it shifts, this page keeps the first phrasing for comparison.

Open at close of session

The porch's calendar case awaits the author's read against its dates (1582, 1752, 1918) and the near-rhyme of eleven minutes with eleven days. The cover clause "each of them this book's argument in another field's clothes" has a quieter substitute on offer. And the datum yield waits its walking-past.
